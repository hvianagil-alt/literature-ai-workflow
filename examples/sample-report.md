# Literature Review: Structured note-taking and recall (EXAMPLE)

> This is a fully fictional worked example. Every paper cited below is made up — see [`examples/README.md`](README.md). Do not cite anything from this file as real research.

## Scope

This example review covers 4 fictional papers on whether structured (e.g. outline-style) note-taking improves recall of technical material compared to unstructured note-taking, published 2021–2024. One paper (Fictional Eli Notincluded, 2020) was excluded as out of scope (wrong domain — creative writing, not technical recall). Full comparison: [`examples/literature-table.md`](literature-table.md).

## Themes

**1. Structured note-taking shows a small, consistent positive effect on recall.** Three of the four papers (Al Researcher 2021, Chen Example 2023's meta-analysis, Dee Sample 2024) find a positive effect in the same direction, though effect sizes are modest (roughly d=0.25–0.4 where reported).

**2. The effect may depend on subject-matter domain.** Bea Scholar 2022 is the one paper that specifically tests domain as a moderator, finding the effect concentrated in humanities material and largely absent for STEM material in their sample. No other paper in this set tests this directly, so it's a single-study finding.

**3. Medium (paper vs. digital) does not appear to change the effect.** Dee Sample 2024 is the only paper testing this, finding the benefit holds in both formats.

## Where the literature agrees

- All three papers that measure a direct effect (Al Researcher 2021, Chen Example 2023, Dee Sample 2024) find structured note-taking associated with better recall than unstructured note-taking, in the same direction.
- No paper in this set found structured note-taking to *hurt* recall.

## Where the literature disagrees

- Bea Scholar 2022's finding of **no effect for STEM material** sits in tension with the generally positive pooled effect in Chen Example 2023's meta-analysis. A plausible reason: Chen Example's meta-analysis aggregates across domains and may average out a real STEM/humanities split that Bea Scholar was specifically powered to detect — but with only one study testing the moderator directly, this is a hypothesis, not a settled disagreement.

## Gaps

- Only one study (Bea Scholar 2022) tests domain as a moderator — this needs replication before treating "structured notes work better for humanities than STEM" as reliable.
- All four studies use short delay windows (immediate to one week). None test whether the effect persists over a semester or longer, which matters more for real study habits than one-week recall.
- Sample sizes are small-to-moderate (N=40–85 per primary study); the meta-analysis's aggregate N≈900 is more reassuring but carries high heterogeneity.

## Implications for our research question

If your question is specifically about technical/STEM material, the current evidence (mainly Bea Scholar 2022) is weaker and more mixed than the general literature suggests — worth treating structured note-taking as "plausibly helpful, not proven" for STEM specifically, and worth designing a new study with domain as an explicit factor rather than assuming the general effect transfers.

## Confidence and caveats

- The overall positive-effect claim rests on 3 primary studies plus 1 meta-analysis of mostly different, unnamed studies — reasonable but not large evidence base.
- The domain-moderation claim (STEM vs. humanities) rests on **a single study** and should be treated as preliminary, not established.
- This is a fictional example dataset; a real review with only 4 papers should state that limitation just as plainly as this one does.
