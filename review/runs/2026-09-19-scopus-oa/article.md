# GLP-1 receptor agonists, vesicle therapeutics, and delivery systems in metabolic disease: a mini-review of a heterogeneous 2026 open-access set

## Abstract

Injectable glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) and dual GIP/GLP-1 agonists are established in type 2 diabetes and obesity. Whether a 2026 gold open-access Scopus export on those drugs, cell-derived vesicles, and delivery systems actually measures comparable constructs is a different question. This mini-review includes seven full texts from a 10-record export. The only pooled human comparative evidence is a Bayesian network meta-analysis in *prediabetes*: among other findings, subcutaneous semaglutide 2.4 mg was associated with a mean weight difference of −13.59 kg versus placebo (95% CI −17.30 to −9.91). Three animal papers attempt non-injectable or local GLP-1–related exposure (pulmonary liraglutide on zinc-oxide carriers; microneedle nanoparticles of an unnamed GLP-1 RA payload in arthritis; niosomes of a plant extract whose metabolic effect was reversed by a GLP-1R antagonist). They do not share a payload, disease, route, or endpoint, and none reports human pharmacokinetics. Extra-glycaemic work in the set is likewise unpooled: semaglutide as a mechanistic probe of PCSK5-dependent post-infarct angiogenesis, and adipose MSC exosomes in a hydrogel for diabetic rat wounds. A patient-reported satisfaction instrument included GLP-1 users but did not estimate drug effect. These papers should not be synthesized as a single delivery, vesicle, or cardiovascular-efficacy result.

## Keywords

GLP-1 receptor agonists; semaglutide; liraglutide; drug delivery; extracellular vesicles; prediabetes; diabetic complications

## 1. Introduction

GLP-1 RAs improve glycaemia and body weight in routine metabolic practice, and dual GIP/GLP-1 agonists extend that pharmacology. That clinical background is not a finding of the papers reviewed here. What this export can test is narrower: in one 2026 open-access Scopus slice tagged to GLP-1 RAs, stem-cell or exosome products, and drug-delivery systems, which of those constructs were actually measured, in whom, and with which endpoints.

Formulation science often aims to avoid subcutaneous injection. Regenerative strategies using mesenchymal stromal cell (MSC) exosomes target diabetic wounds and other complications. Extra-glycaemic actions, including cardiovascular repair, are a third active thread. If those threads were empirically aligned, a short review could report a delivery effect, a vesicle effect, and a repair effect. They are not aligned in this set. The argument below is therefore about *incommensurability*: one human comparative analysis in prediabetes, a cluster of unlike preclinical exposure experiments, and two mechanistic or device papers that do not test metabolic outcomes.

This article is a mini-review of the seven readable full texts, not a comprehensive survey of GLP-1 RAs or extracellular vesicles. Search, eligibility, and selection are in Section 2. Section 3 treats the only human comparative evidence. Sections 4–6 separate delivery engineering, extra-glycaemic repair, and patient-reported satisfaction because those constructs were measured apart.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Search and sources

Records were those in a Scopus BibTeX export dated 19 September 2026 (10 articles, all 2026, all tagged gold open access by Scopus). No additional databases were searched for this write-up.

### 2.2 Eligibility

A paper was included if the full text addressed GLP-1 or a related incretin drug, a stem-cell or exosome product, and/or a drug-delivery system, in metabolic disease or a closely related inflammatory or tissue-repair setting. Papers with no such link, unreadable files, and files that could not be retrieved were excluded from the evidence base.

### 2.3 Study selection

Of 10 records, one was excluded on topic (yeast plasmid engineering). Nine full texts were sought; one gold open-access Elsevier article (exosome-mimetic nanovesicles in diabetic cerebral infarction) could not be downloaded and is not cited as evidence. Eight full texts were assessed; one mixed-methods evaluation of a digital behavioural weight programme was excluded because GLP-1 drugs were not the intervention. **Seven studies were included.**

## 3. The only human comparative evidence in this set is in prediabetes

If the question is what incretin drugs *do in people*, this export contains one paper. Wu et al. performed a Bayesian network meta-analysis of randomized trials of at least 12 weeks in adults with prediabetes, searching to 29 March 2025 [1]. Fifty-five trials (16,610 participants; 37 interventions) contributed to the network [1]. Versus placebo, most included drugs reduced HbA1c (mean difference −0.94 to −0.27%), fasting plasma glucose (−26.42 to −0.15 mg/dL), weight (−13.59 to −5.99 kg), and BMI (−4.50 to −0.08 kg/m²) [1]. Subcutaneous semaglutide 2.4 mg had the largest highlighted weight effect (−13.59 kg; 95% CI −17.30 to −9.91) and an HbA1c difference of −0.39% (−0.55 to −0.25) [1]. Tirzepatide 15 mg reduced fasting glucose by −9.58 mg/dL (−12.00 to −7.15) [1]. The authors judged GLP-1 RAs, GIP/GLP-1 RAs, and thiazolidinediones favourably, and named 2.4 mg semaglutide and 15 mg tirzepatide as the strongest options among included interventions for glucose and BMI [1]. They report no overall difference in adverse-event rates across interventions, with higher serious adverse events for sitagliptin 100 mg in their analysis [1]. Stated limitations include few trials per class, heterogeneity, follow-up from 12 to 120 weeks, and incomplete safety data [1].

Three limits follow immediately for the rest of this review. First, the population is prediabetes, not type 2 diabetes or obesity-as-indication. Second, the analysis compares licensed (and other) pharmacologic nodes; it does not evaluate pulmonary, transdermal, or niosomal delivery. Third, its safety statement cannot be borrowed to underwrite zinc-oxide inhalation or an exosome hydrogel. Those are different questions, asked in different papers.

## 4. Non-injectable GLP-1-related exposure is an engineering problem without a shared endpoint

The remaining GLP-1-related experimental papers do not test whether incretin drugs work. They test whether a GLP-1-related signal can be presented to tissue by a route other than conventional subcutaneous injection—or, in one case, whether a botanical nanocarrier acts through GLP-1R. Grouping them as “delivery systems” is useful only as a translational gap. It would be incorrect as a pooled efficacy class.

Jeong et al. asked a pharmacokinetic question in rats: can zinc-oxide morphology be chosen so that inhalable liraglutide deposits distally, crosses mucus, evades macrophages, and approaches subcutaneous bioavailability [2]. Of three shapes, compact spiky ZO-3 particles (1–3 μm with short tips) showed aerodynamic behaviour favouring distal deposition, greater mucus penetration, and lower macrophage uptake [2]. After intratracheal liraglutide-loaded ZO-3, relative bioavailability versus subcutaneous injection was about 60% in healthy rats and 51% in alloxan-diabetic rats (pharmacokinetic n = 4 per group) [2]. The authors contrast this with the <2% absolute bioavailability of marketed oral semaglutide; that comparison is to product literature, not an oral arm in the same animals [2]. Zinc-oxide pulmonary residence was reported as largely cleared within 48 hours; longer-term safety was not established [2]. The endpoint is relative pulmonary bioavailability of *liraglutide* in a small-n rat PK design. It is not a glycaemic outcomes result, and it is not human inhalable GLP-1.

Zhang et al. asked a different exposure question in a different disease [3]. Inefficient articular delivery after *systemic* GLP-1 RA dosing is the gap they name; the model is collagen-induced arthritis, not diabetes [3]. Silk-fibroin nanoparticles bearing a type II collagen-binding peptide (WYRGRL) were loaded with a payload referred to as “GLP-1RAs” in the extracted abstract and placed in dissolving microneedles [3]. Encapsulation efficiency was about 28% and 33% at silk:drug mass ratios of 5:1 and 10:1 (no statistically significant difference between those ratios); about 60% of payload was released by day 14 [3]. In CIA rats the authors report joint accumulation, suppression of chondrocyte ferroptosis via cGAS-STING, macrophage M2 polarization, and attenuated disease [3]. Some biodistribution arms used n = 6 per group [3]. Two features block any pooling with Jeong. The disease setting is inflammatory joint disease, allowed by this review’s protocol as a related inflammatory context, but it is not metabolic disease. The molecule is not named in the extracted abstract, so the paper cannot be read as a liraglutide or semaglutide formulation study on the basis of that text.

Khater et al. sit next to these papers only because the phenotype was GLP-1R-dependent, not because the product is a GLP-1 RA [4]. *Phyllanthus niruri* extract was loaded into niosomes (PHYLN-NIO: 225.1 ± 19.6 nm; encapsulation 73.5 ± 4.2%) and given orally at 100 mg/kg in high-fat-diet rats (n = 8 per group; seven groups including semaglutide ± exendin 9–39) [4]. PHYLN-NIO improved body weight, hepatic steatosis, inflammation, oxidative stress, insulin sensitivity, and lipids relative to diet alone [4]. Benefit was reversed by the GLP-1R antagonist exendin 9–39, which the authors interpret as GLP-1R dependence [4]. Semaglutide was the licensed-agonist comparator, not the formulated payload [4]. Reading Khater as “niosomal GLP-1 RA delivery” would confuse a botanical extract with a peptide drug. Reading Jeong’s product-literature oral-semaglutide remark and Khater’s oral niosomes as a disagreement about oral GLP-1 bioavailability would make the same category error: the payloads are not the same.

What these three papers jointly support is modest and negative in the best sense: in this set, non-injectable or local GLP-1-related exposure remains preclinical, and the experiments are not replicates of one another. They cannot be averaged, ranked, or translated into a clinical bioavailability claim.

## 5. Extra-glycaemic and regenerative work here is mechanistic, not metabolic-outcomes evidence

A second cluster is tissue repair. It is not a second GLP-1 delivery platform, and it is not one vesicle class.

Guo et al. identified PCSK5 as up-regulated after myocardial infarction in human and mouse datasets and showed that endothelial PCSK5 supports post-infarct angiogenesis and repair [5]. They further report that semaglutide promoted angiogenesis and tissue repair in a partly PCSK5-dependent manner, including ETS1-linked induction of PCSK5 in endothelial cells [5]. Human plasma sample sizes in the reported panels are in the tens (for example n = 28–30 for some ELISA comparisons; a correlation cohort n = 42) [5]. This is mechanistic cardiology. It does not estimate cardiovascular event rates in diabetes, and it does not corroborate Wu’s prediabetes network—the roles of semaglutide (NMA node versus mechanistic probe) are complementary, not confirmatory [1,5].

Chen et al. combined adipose-derived MSC exosomes with a *Spirulina platensis*/chlorophyll-derivative hydrogel, with or without laser, in a diabetic rat skin-wound model [6]. Wound transcriptomes showed fewer endothelial cells and M2 macrophages than healthy skin [6]. In vitro, the hydrogel inhibited biofilm, was antimicrobial, and promoted angiogenesis and M2 polarization [6]. In vivo, the laser combination was reported to increase proliferative and angiogenic proteins, shift macrophages toward M2, reduce inflammation, and accelerate closure [6]. Group sizes were not stated in the extracted text and are not invented here [6]. The product is a vesicle-plus-device strategy for a diabetic *complication*. It contains no GLP-1 RA, so it cannot be used to claim that incretin drugs heal wounds, and Guo’s endothelial PCSK5 pathway cannot be used to explain Chen’s hydrogel.

Zhang et al. also report a repair-adjacent mechanism (chondrocyte ferroptosis, macrophage polarization), but that work was already an articular-delivery experiment in RA [3]. Citing it again as “regenerative GLP-1” would double-count one CIA-rat study and imply a class that this set does not define.

The missing Huang paper would have sat nearer Chen than Guo on product type (exosome-mimetic nanovesicles in a diabetic cerebral complication). It was not retrieved, so vesicle conclusions in this review rest on a single multi-component rat wound study with unstated group sizes [6].

## 6. Satisfaction with diabetes medicines is a different endpoint

Boye et al. developed the Patient Satisfaction with Medication for Diabetes (PSMD) questionnaire because existing instruments may miss attributes of newer medicines, including GLP-1 RAs [7]. Concept elicitation included 10 adults with type 1 diabetes and 20 with type 2; cognitive interviews included 5 and 10 respectively [7]. In the type 2 cognitive sample, 30% used oral medication plus an injectable GLP-1 RA and 10% used a dual GIP/GLP-1 RA [7]. Patients named effectiveness, administration, and effects on energy, weight, and eating behaviour as contributors to satisfaction [7]. The paper supports content validity only [7]. It does not estimate glycaemic or weight efficacy, does not supply reliability or construct-validity coefficients, and does not test a delivery device.

The scientific point is not that Boye is peripheral in a pejorative sense. It is that *satisfaction* is a construct none of the efficacy, PK, or wound papers measured. Wu’s network does not tell us whether people prefer 2.4 mg subcutaneous semaglutide; Boye’s interviews do not tell us whether that dose reduces weight. A review that folded Boye into “GLP-1 works” would be overclaiming; a review that omitted it would hide the only patient-reported measurement in the set.

## 7. Discussion

Three messages are stable across these seven papers.

First, **incretin pharmacology in people is represented by a single network meta-analysis in prediabetes** [1]. That analysis is the only source of pooled human effect sizes in the set. Delivery, vesicle, and cardiac papers are animal, cellular, or mixed human-observational mechanistic work [2–6] and cannot be read as clinical bioavailability, wound-healing proof in patients, or cardiovascular-outcome evidence.

Second, **non-injectable GLP-1-related exposure is being attempted by several routes, but each paper uses a different payload, species, and endpoint** [2–4]. Zhang et al. study RA joints rather than metabolic disease [3]. Khater et al. study a plant extract [4]. Treating them as one delivery platform would be a forced theme.

Third, **semaglutide appears in three roles that do not validate one another**: as an NMA node [1], as a positive-control GLP-1 RA beside a botanical niosome [4], and as a mechanistic probe in cardiac repair [5]. Those roles are complementary.

Disagreements in this set are almost entirely differences of methods, population, and endpoint rather than conflicting estimates of one quantity. Jeong’s oral-semaglutide bioavailability remark and Khater’s oral niosomes are not a dispute about oral GLP-1 RA pharmacokinetics. Wu’s finding of no overall difference in adverse-event incidence among NMA interventions [1] does not contradict Jeong’s statement that long-term pulmonary ZnO safety needs further study [2].

Gaps that follow from this sample, rather than from the wider literature, include: no retrieved full text for an otherwise eligible exosome-mimetic stroke paper; no head-to-head clinical comparison of pulmonary or microneedle GLP-1 RA delivery with subcutaneous dosing; no named GLP-1 RA molecule in the extracted Zhang abstract [3]; unstated animal n in the Chen extract [6]; and no quantitative psychometric evaluation of PSMD [7]. This export also cannot answer comparative effectiveness of incretin drugs in type 2 diabetes, because that population was not Wu’s [1].

## 8. Conclusions

In this open-access 2026 slice, pooled human evidence that incretin drugs improve weight and glycaemia *in prediabetes* comes from Wu et al. [1]. Work on pulmonary liraglutide, microneedle GLP-1 RA nanoparticles, niosomal GLP-1R agonism by a botanical extract, and MSC-exosome hydrogels remains preclinical and unpooled [2–4,6]. Semaglutide’s link to PCSK5-dependent angiogenesis is a mechanistic finding [5]. Patient satisfaction with diabetes medicines, including GLP-1 RAs, was explored only as content validity [7]. Claims in this review are limited to those seven full texts.
