# GLP-1 receptor agonists, vesicle therapeutics, and delivery systems in metabolic disease: a mini-review of a 2026 open-access Scopus set

## Abstract

Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) are established in type 2 diabetes and obesity, while new work explores non-injectable delivery, extra-glycaemic biology, and cell-derived vesicles for diabetic complications. This mini-review synthesizes seven full texts retrieved from a 10-record gold open-access Scopus export (2026). Human comparative efficacy in this set comes from one Bayesian network meta-analysis in prediabetes: among other findings, subcutaneous semaglutide 2.4 mg was associated with a mean weight difference of −13.59 kg versus placebo (95% CI −17.30 to −9.91). Non-injectable GLP-1 RA delivery is represented only in animals (pulmonary liraglutide on zinc-oxide carriers; microneedle-nanoparticle GLP-1 RAs in arthritis; niosomes of a plant extract acting through GLP-1R). Adipose MSC exosomes in a hydrogel were studied for diabetic wounds. Semaglutide also appears as a mechanistic probe in post-infarct angiogenesis. A patient-reported satisfaction instrument included GLP-1 users but did not estimate drug effect. These papers do not share a common endpoint; they should not be pooled as a single “delivery effect.”

## Keywords

GLP-1 receptor agonists; semaglutide; liraglutide; drug delivery; extracellular vesicles; prediabetes; diabetic complications

## 1. Introduction

GLP-1 RAs improve glycaemia and body weight, and dual GIP/GLP-1 agonists extend that pharmacology. In parallel, formulation science aims to avoid subcutaneous injection (oral, pulmonary, transdermal), and regenerative approaches using mesenchymal stromal cell (MSC) exosomes target diabetic wounds and other complications. Extra-glycaemic actions, including cardiovascular repair, are another active thread.

This article reviews the papers that could be read in full from one open-access Scopus export on those themes. It is a mini-review of that set, not a comprehensive survey of the field. Search, eligibility, and selection are summarized in Section 2; the rest of the paper is organized by theme.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Search and sources

Records were those in a Scopus BibTeX export dated 19 September 2026 (10 articles, all 2026, all tagged gold open access by Scopus). No additional databases were searched for this write-up.

### 2.2 Eligibility

A paper was included if the full text addressed GLP-1 or a related incretin drug, a stem-cell or exosome product, and/or a drug-delivery system, in metabolic disease or a closely related inflammatory or tissue-repair setting. Papers with no such link, unreadable files, and files that could not be retrieved were excluded.

### 2.3 Study selection

Of 10 records, one was excluded on topic (yeast plasmid engineering). Nine full texts were sought; one gold open-access Elsevier article could not be downloaded and is not cited as evidence. Eight full texts were assessed; one mixed-methods evaluation of a digital behavioural weight programme was excluded because GLP-1 drugs were not the intervention. **Seven studies were included.**

## 3. Non-injectable and locally targeted GLP-1 RA delivery

### 3.1 Pulmonary delivery of liraglutide

Jeong et al. fabricated morphology-controlled zinc-oxide (ZO) carriers for inhalable liraglutide [1]. Three shapes were compared; the compact spiky ZO-3 particles (1–3 μm with short tips) showed aerodynamic behaviour favouring distal deposition, greater mucus penetration, and lower macrophage uptake [1]. After intratracheal administration of liraglutide-loaded ZO-3, relative bioavailability versus subcutaneous injection was about 60% in healthy rats and 51% in alloxan-diabetic rats (pharmacokinetic n = 4 per group) [1]. The authors contrast this with the <2% absolute bioavailability of marketed oral semaglutide; that comparison is to product literature, not an oral arm in the same animals [1]. Zinc-oxide pulmonary residence was reported as largely cleared within 48 hours; longer-term safety was not established [1].

### 3.2 Microneedle delivery of GLP-1 RA nanoparticles to joints

Zhang et al. addressed poor articular exposure after systemic GLP-1 RA dosing in rheumatoid arthritis (RA), not diabetes [2]. Silk-fibroin nanoparticles bearing a type II collagen-binding peptide (WYRGRL) were loaded with GLP-1 RAs and placed in dissolving microneedles [2]. Encapsulation efficiency was about 28% and 33% at silk:drug mass ratios of 5:1 and 10:1 (no statistically significant difference between those ratios); about 60% of payload was released by day 14 [2]. In collagen-induced arthritis rats the authors report joint accumulation, suppression of chondrocyte ferroptosis via cGAS-STING, macrophage M2 polarization, and attenuated disease [2]. The specific GLP-1 RA molecule is named only as “GLP-1RAs” in the abstract; the work is preclinical [2].

### 3.3 Niosomes that act through GLP-1R

Khater et al. loaded *Phyllanthus niruri* extract into niosomes (PHYLN-NIO: 225.1 ± 19.6 nm; encapsulation 73.5 ± 4.2%) and compared them with semaglutide in high-fat-diet rats (n = 8 per group) [3]. PHYLN-NIO improved body weight, hepatic steatosis, inflammation, oxidative stress, insulin sensitivity, and lipids relative to diet alone [3]. Benefit was reversed by the GLP-1R antagonist exendin 9–39, which the authors interpret as GLP-1R dependence [3]. This is a botanical nanocarrier with a GLP-1R mechanism, not a licensed GLP-1 RA product [3].

## 4. Clinical efficacy of incretin-based drugs in prediabetes

Wu et al. performed a Bayesian network meta-analysis of randomized trials (≥12 weeks) in adults with prediabetes [4]. Fifty-five trials (16,610 participants; 37 interventions) were included after a search to 29 March 2025 [4]. Versus placebo, most drugs reduced HbA1c (mean difference −0.94 to −0.27%), fasting plasma glucose (−26.42 to −0.15 mg/dL), weight (−13.59 to −5.99 kg), and BMI (−4.50 to −0.08 kg/m²) [4]. Subcutaneous semaglutide 2.4 mg had the largest highlighted weight effect (−13.59 kg; 95% CI −17.30 to −9.91) and an HbA1c difference of −0.39% (−0.55 to −0.25) [4]. Tirzepatide 15 mg reduced fasting glucose by −9.58 mg/dL (−12.00 to −7.15) [4]. The authors judged GLP-1 RAs, GIP/GLP-1 RAs, and thiazolidinediones favourably, and named 2.4 mg semaglutide and 15 mg tirzepatide as the strongest options among included interventions for glucose and BMI [4]. They report no overall difference in adverse-event rates across interventions, with higher serious adverse events for sitagliptin 100 mg in their analysis [4]. Stated limitations include few trials per class, heterogeneity, follow-up from 12 to 120 weeks, and incomplete safety data [4].

This is the only paper in the set that supplies pooled human effect sizes for incretin drugs.

## 5. Mesenchymal stromal cell exosomes in diabetic wound repair

Chen et al. combined adipose-derived MSC exosomes with a *Spirulina platensis*/chlorophyll-derivative hydrogel, with or without laser, in a diabetic rat skin-wound model [5]. Wound transcriptomes showed fewer endothelial cells and M2 macrophages than healthy skin [5]. In vitro, the hydrogel inhibited biofilm, was antimicrobial, and promoted angiogenesis and M2 polarization [5]. In vivo, the laser combination was reported to increase proliferative and angiogenic proteins, shift macrophages toward M2, reduce inflammation, and accelerate closure [5]. Group sizes were not stated in the extracted text and are not invented here [5]. This is a vesicle-plus-device strategy for a diabetic complication, not a GLP-1 formulation [5].

## 6. Extra-glycaemic biology: semaglutide and cardiac repair

Guo et al. identified PCSK5 as up-regulated after myocardial infarction in human and mouse datasets and showed that endothelial PCSK5 supports post-infarct angiogenesis and repair [6]. They further report that semaglutide promoted angiogenesis and tissue repair in a partly PCSK5-dependent manner, including ETS1-linked induction of PCSK5 in endothelial cells [6]. Human plasma sample sizes in the reported panels are in the tens (for example n = 28–30 for some ELISA comparisons) [6]. This is mechanistic cardiology, not a diabetes outcomes trial [6].

## 7. Patient-reported satisfaction with diabetes medicines including GLP-1 RAs

Boye et al. developed the Patient Satisfaction with Medication for Diabetes (PSMD) questionnaire because existing instruments may miss attributes of newer medicines [7]. Concept elicitation included 10 adults with type 1 diabetes and 20 with type 2; cognitive interviews included 5 and 10 respectively [7]. In the type 2 cognitive sample, 30% used oral medication plus an injectable GLP-1 RA and 10% used a dual GIP/GLP-1 RA [7]. The paper supports content validity only; it does not estimate glycaemic or weight efficacy [7].

## 8. Discussion

Across these seven papers, three messages are stable.

First, **incretin pharmacology in people** is represented by a single network meta-analysis in prediabetes [4]. Delivery and vesicle studies are animal or in vitro [1–3,5] and cannot be read as clinical bioavailability or wound-healing proof in patients.

Second, **non-injectable GLP-1 RA exposure** is being attempted by several routes (lung, skin, oral niosomes), but each paper uses a different payload, species, and endpoint [1–3]. Zhang et al. study RA joints rather than metabolic disease [2]. Treating them as one delivery platform would be incorrect.

Third, **semaglutide is used in three different roles**: as an NMA node [4], as a positive-control GLP-1 RA in a niosome study [3], and as a mechanistic probe in cardiac repair [6]. Those roles are complementary, not confirmatory of one another.

Gaps that follow from this set, rather than from the wider literature, include: no retrieved full text for an otherwise eligible exosome-mimetic stroke paper; no head-to-head clinical comparison of pulmonary or microneedle GLP-1 RA delivery with subcutaneous dosing; and no validated quantitative use of the PSMD instrument in a trial [7].

## 9. Conclusions

In this open-access 2026 slice, pooled human evidence that incretin drugs improve weight and glycaemia in prediabetes comes from Wu et al. [4]. Work on pulmonary liraglutide, microneedle GLP-1 RA nanoparticles, niosomal GLP-1R agonism, and MSC-exosome hydrogels remains preclinical [1–3,5]. Semaglutide’s link to PCSK5-dependent angiogenesis is a mechanistic finding [6]. Claims in this review are limited to those seven full texts.
