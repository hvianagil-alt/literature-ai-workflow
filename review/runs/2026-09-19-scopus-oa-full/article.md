# GLP-1 receptor agonists, vesicle products, and delivery systems in a 2024–2026 Scopus open-access export: a documented review

## Abstract

This article reviews the papers that could be read in full from a complete Scopus gold/open-access BibTeX export (459 records, 19 September 2026) on glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs), stem-cell/exosome products, and drug-delivery systems in metabolic disease and closely related settings. Titles were screened with pre-specified keywords (185 sought). Public PDFs were requested only from open-access endpoints; 139 eligible titles had no downloadable public PDF and are not cited as evidence. Forty-six files were assessed; two were excluded (one off-topic inflammatory-bowel delivery paper; one file whose body was a different journal article). **Forty-four studies from the export were included**, plus one gap-fill OA paper on oral GLP-1 tablet engineering (Pechenov et al., 2021). They do not share a primary endpoint. Human comparative data in this retrieved set include a type 1 diabetes crossover trial of weekly semaglutide plus automated insulin delivery (time in range 74.2% vs 69.4% on placebo; mean difference 4.8 percentage points; 24 completers of 28 randomized) and a 50-trial safety meta-analysis of semaglutide in people at raised cardiovascular risk (54,972 participants; all-cause mortality RR 0.85, 95% CI 0.79–0.91). Non-injectable GLP-1 RA delivery remains largely preclinical (pulmonary liraglutide–zinc oxide; microneedle semaglutide or GLP-1 RA nanoparticles; oral lipid nanocapsules and self-emulsifying systems). One adipose-MSC exosome hydrogel was studied in diabetic wounds. This is an auditable reading of one OA export, not a field-wide systematic review.

## Keywords

GLP-1 receptor agonists; semaglutide; liraglutide; drug delivery; extracellular vesicles; open access; PRISMA

## 1. Introduction

GLP-1 RAs and dual GIP/GLP-1 agonists are now central to type 2 diabetes and obesity care. In parallel, formulation science tries to avoid subcutaneous injection, and cell-derived vesicles are tested for diabetic complications. A keyword export from Scopus mixes those themes with off-topic OA hits. This paper answers a narrower question: **what is in this 459-record open-access export after public PDFs are fetched and eligibility is applied?**

The protocol was written before retrieval. One additional open-access full text (Pechenov et al., 2021) was retrieved after the table to contextualize oral GLP-1 bioavailability; it was not in the original export.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Search and sources

No new database search was run. Identification is the user-uploaded Scopus BibTeX file (`identification.bib`; header “EXPORT DATE: 19 September 2026”). All 459 `@ARTICLE` records were parsed; the file has **no abstracts**, so title screening used titles only.

### 2.2 Eligibility

A record was **included** if the title, then the retrieved full text, addressed at least one of: (i) GLP-1 / a GLP-1 RA / related incretin drugs; (ii) stem cells, MSCs, or exosomes/EVs as therapy or carrier; (iii) a drug-delivery system, **and** the setting was metabolic disease or a closely related inflammatory or tissue-repair context. Borderline titles were kept for full text.

**Excluded:** no such link; PDF not retrieved; unreadable or wrong-file PDF.

### 2.3 Retrieval

Public PDFs were requested from open-access endpoints (OpenAlex, Unpaywall, Europe PMC, publisher conventions, doi.org). Files were kept only if the body started with `%PDF-`. Paywalled and pirate sources were not used. Many Scopus-tagged OA articles (especially Elsevier, MDPI, Wiley) returned no public PDF body; those titles are listed in the PRISMA file and are not used as evidence.

### 2.4 Extraction and synthesis

Retrieved PDFs were converted locally to text. Structured notes copy the abstract/lead and flag missing numbers as **not stated**. The comparison table was built from those notes. Factual sentences below map to extracted text or to the PRISMA/fetch logs. Two protocols without outcome data are named as protocols, not as efficacy results.

## 3. Clinical use of incretin drugs

### 3.1 Glycaemia, adjunct insulin, and oral products

Pasqua et al. randomized 28 adults with type 1 diabetes in a double-blind crossover of weekly subcutaneous semaglutide (up to 1 mg or maximum tolerated dose) versus placebo as an adjunct to automated insulin delivery; 24 completed both periods [1]. Time in the target glucose range was 74.2% (SD 9.7) on semaglutide versus 69.4% (SD 10.4) on placebo (difference 4.8 percentage points; *P* = 0.006) [1]. Hypoglycaemia was not increased in the authors’ primary comparison [1]. This is the only fully reported randomized glycaemic trial among the PDFs we could open.

Chen et al. retrospectively matched 202 patients with type 2 diabetes; after 3–6 months, time in range was 76.0% with GLP-1 RA-based therapy versus 65.7% with other oral agents (*p* < 0.05) [2]. That is observational CGM data in type 2 diabetes, not a second test of Pasqua’s type 1 automated-delivery design [1,2].

Hirotsu et al. switched Japanese adults with type 2 diabetes from DPP-4 inhibitors to oral semaglutide and asked who reached HbA1c <7% [3]. The extracted methods report 74 people enrolled; 44 reached the target (3 mg n=7; 7 mg n=24; 14 mg n=13) and 17 did not [3]. That is a predictor/pilot analysis, not a placebo-controlled efficacy trial.

Nielsen et al. tested bioequivalence of a second-generation oral semaglutide tablet (1.5, 4, and 9 mg) against the first-generation 3/7/14 mg product in healthy participants and report that PMDA bioequivalence criteria were met [4]. Ma et al. studied oral **orforglipron**, a non-peptide GLP-1 RA: geometric mean AUC and Cmax were about 18–24% lower when dosed fed versus fasted, without a corresponding efficacy comparison in that food-effect package [5]. Neither paper is a head-to-head outcome trial against injectable semaglutide [4,5].

Swift et al. characterized real-world lines of therapy, time on each line, and reasons for ending a line among people with type 2 diabetes who started oral semaglutide [6]. Sample size is **not stated** in the extracted lead [6]. Xiong et al. mined FAERS (2019–2023) for adverse-event reports after oral semaglutide tablets; they present a safety-signal study, not incidence from a defined cohort [7]. Abdallah et al. evaluated GLP-1 RA use for insufficient weight loss or weight regain after bariatric surgery; the extracted lead states the aim but not n or an effect size [8]. Wang et al. examined racial and ethnic differences in SGLT2 inhibitor and GLP-1 RA uptake among US Medicare beneficiaries with type 2 diabetes and heart failure, atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, or chronic kidney disease (2013–2019) [9]. Ali et al. used a microsimulation of introducing SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 RAs in low- and middle-income countries, asking what that would do to insulin use and complications [10]. These five papers [6–10] answer utilisation, pharmacovigilance, access, and modeled policy questions. They cannot be pooled with Pasqua [1] or Chen [2].

Lunati et al. report two young women with latent autoimmune diabetes of youth treated with oral semaglutide plus basal insulin (case series; one described as a 20-year-old with BMI 31.8 kg/m² and residual C-peptide) [11]. That is not a trial estimate [11].

Two included files are **protocols without results**. Fragozo-Ramos et al. describe AID-JUNCT: GIP/GLP-1 RA as adjunct to automated insulin delivery in adults with type 1 diabetes, noting that 20–30% of adults reach A1c <7% and that RCTs of dual agonists in this setting remain insufficient [12]. Tian, Zhang, Zhang et al. describe a randomized protocol of transcutaneous auricular vagus-nerve stimulation plus semaglutide, aimed at gastrointestinal side effects and efficacy; no outcomes are in that file [13].

### 3.2 Safety at cardiovascular risk and narrative clinical reviews

Sillassen et al. meta-analysed 50 placebo-controlled trials of semaglutide in people at increased cardiovascular risk (54,972 participants randomized; 30,281 semaglutide, 24,691 placebo) [14]. They report lower all-cause mortality (RR 0.85; 95% CI 0.79–0.91; *I*² = 0%), myocardial infarction (RR 0.77; 0.69–0.85), and serious adverse events (RR 0.93; 0.88–0.98), with higher non-serious gastrointestinal events including nausea (RR 3.00; 2.63–3.42), vomiting (RR 4.12; 3.47–4.90), and diarrhoea (RR 1.88; 1.68–2.11) [14]. Source CVOT full texts (SELECT, SUSTAIN-6) were **not** retrieved as extra PDFs for this export review; Sillassen is the safety synthesis we could actually open [14].

Four narrative reviews in the export are background, not new trials. Bonga and Padhan compare liraglutide, semaglutide, and tirzepatide for weight reduction in people with obesity who do not have diabetes, including cost-effectiveness comments in the extracted lead [15]. Son reviews GLP-1-based obesity pharmacology and states that liraglutide, semaglutide, and tirzepatide can induce substantial weight loss (the review’s own range is about 10–25%) with additional cardiorenal claims; those percentages are Son’s synthesis, not a meta-analysis we recomputed [16]. Wallentowitz et al. review incretin-based therapy in maternal obesity and metabolic syndrome, including how GLP-1 and dual GIP/GLP-1 agonists might interact with pregnancy physiology; they do not report a pregnancy RCT in this extract [17]. Zachou et al. review anti-diabetic drugs in NAFLD/NASH, noting ~30% global NAFLD prevalence in their purpose statement and arguing that insulin resistance makes anti-diabetic drugs a rational NAFLD strategy [18]. None of [15–18] should be read as primary effect sizes from this export.

## 4. Non-injectable and locally targeted delivery

### 4.1 Pulmonary, transdermal, and articular GLP-1 RAs

Jeong et al. compared three zinc-oxide particle shapes as inhalable carriers for liraglutide [19]. After intratracheal LG@ZO-3 they report relative bioavailability of about 60% versus subcutaneous injection in healthy rats and 51% in alloxan-diabetic rats, contrasting that with <2% oral bioavailability of marketed oral semaglutide (product literature, not an oral arm in the same animals) [19].

Woo et al. made dissolving hyaluronic acid/aminoclay microneedles carrying a semaglutide–aminoclay nanocomplex and report, in rats, reductions in glucose, HbA1c, lipids, intake, and body weight; exact group sizes are **not stated** in the extracted abstract [20]. Yao et al. describe an adipose-targeted microneedle co-delivering semaglutide and rosiglitazone aimed at adipose browning; quantitative animal outcomes are **not stated** in the extracted lead [21]. Zhang et al. loaded GLP-1 RAs into silk-fibroin nanoparticles (encapsulation about 28–33%; about 60% released by day 14) inside dissolving microneedles for collagen-induced arthritis in rats, not diabetes; the payload is named as “GLP-1RAs” in the abstract [22].

### 4.2 Oral nanocarriers, permeation enhancers, and peptide engineering

Domingues et al. gave oral semaglutide-loaded lipid nanocapsules intended both to raise native GLP-1 secretion and to absorb encapsulated semaglutide in MASLD models [23]. Sandmeier et al. compared reverse micelles versus hydrophobic ion pairs for loading semaglutide into self-emulsifying systems (droplet sizes 50–300 nm in the extracted lead) [24]. Kweon et al. report a liraglutide nanoformulation aimed at ASBT/EGFR-mediated intestinal absorption [25]. Dangi and Sharma describe a modified-release semaglutide dosage form (FPR-8) intended to flatten a wide plasma-concentration range (5.85–38.14 in the unformulated solution, units **not restated** in the lead) [26].

As a gap-fill (not in the original export), Pechenov et al. engineered MEDI7219, an oral GLP-1 RA tablet, and report about 6% bioavailability in dogs—higher than the 0.4–1% range they cite for many oral peptides, and a different product and species than Jeong’s product-literature contrast with oral semaglutide [45].

Four included papers sit in a metabolic delivery setting but do **not** share a GLP-1 RA clinical endpoint. Fredholt et al. co-formulated insulin with sodium caprate (C10, 26 mg/kg) as mini-tablets versus liquid in rats; a 75 IU/kg insulin mini-tablet with C10 increased bioavailability 26-fold in the extracted lead, with transient mucosal effects [27]. Klotsman et al. implanted an 84-day exenatide system (OKV-119) in five cats and detected plasma exenatide through day 84 [28]. Singh et al. review PLGA microspheres as a future insulin depot, not a new GLP-1 trial [29]. Yu et al. acetylated a long-acting insulin analogue and compared it with insulin icodec in a preclinical design [30]. Xu et al. biosynthesized tirzepatide variants carrying D-amino acids and ornithines in *E. coli*, aimed at stability/oral-delivery problems of the dual agonist, not at a human outcome [31].

## 5. Extra-glycaemic biology, complications, and public interest

da Silva Lima et al. report that GLP-1 and GIP agonism, at concentrations that stimulate insulin release, did **not** change lipid content in human hepatocytes or activation of human hepatic stellate cells—evidence against a direct hepatic action of those agonists in their assays [32]. Hölscher reviews GLP-1/GIP signalling in Alzheimer and Parkinson disease as a possible disease-modifying strategy [33].

Shen et al. combine Mendelian randomization with a systematic review of GLP-1 RAs and diabetic retinopathy risk in European-ancestry data [34]. Yue et al. analyse FAERS for semaglutide and diabetic retinopathy and add a preclinical retinal-endothelial assay [35]. Abo-Elenin et al. report an osteoprotective effect of semaglutide in ovariectomized rats, with β-catenin as a stated mechanism in the title/lead [36]. Tian, Liu, Bao, and Li report that semaglutide increased BMSC proliferation, ALP activity, and RUNX2/OCN expression via Wnt/LRP5/β-catenin in a cell study; animal n is **not stated** in that extract [37]. Sun et al. report metabolic and gut-microbiota effects of semaglutide in high-fat-diet mice, including faecal microbiota transfer arms [38]. Qi et al. link GABA_B receptor agonism to blunting GLP-1 RA chronotropy and post-infarct arrhythmia in a preclinical model [39]. Yang et al. report that electroacupuncture raised semaglutide exposure and reduced β-cell apoptosis in a type 2 diabetes model (dose-sparing claim in the title; numerical PK values **not stated** in the extracted lead) [40]. Mastaitis et al. show that blocking GDF8 and activin A spared muscle and enhanced fat loss during GLP-1 treatment in obese male mice and non-human primates [41].

Raubenheimer et al. analysed Google Trends for worldwide interest in semaglutide during supply shortages; that is infodemiology, not a treatment trial [42]. Khater et al. loaded *Phyllanthus niruri* into niosomes (225.1 ± 19.6 nm; encapsulation 73.5 ± 4.2%; n = 8/group in the full methods of that paper) and report GLP-1R-dependent benefit reversed by exendin 9–39, with semaglutide as a comparator in high-fat-diet rats [43].

## 6. Mesenchymal stromal cell exosomes in diabetic wounds

Chen et al. combined adipose-derived MSC exosomes with a *Spirulina* hydrogel, with or without laser, in a diabetic rat wound model [44]. They report biofilm inhibition, M2/angiogenic shifts, and faster closure with the laser combination [44]. Group sizes were **not stated** in the extracted text [44]. This is a vesicle-plus-device strategy for a diabetic complication, not a GLP-1 formulation [44].

## 7. Discussion

Three points follow from the **retrieved** papers, not from the unread OA titles.

First, **human drug-effect estimates in this set are thin and mixed**. The only randomized, fully reported glycaemic trial among the PDFs we could open is Pasqua et al. in type 1 diabetes with automated insulin delivery [1]. Pooled safety/mortality estimates for semaglutide come from Sillassen et al. [14]. Real-world TIR, FAERS, Medicare, and LMIC microsimulation papers answer different questions [2,7,9,10]. They cannot be pooled with the delivery papers.

Second, **non-injectable GLP-1 RA exposure is still mostly animal or in vitro here**: lung [19], skin [20–22], oral nanocapsules/SEDDS [23,24], and botanical niosomes acting through GLP-1R [43]. Zhang et al. study RA joints [22]. Treating them as one “delivery effect” would be incorrect.

Third, **retrieval, not title screening, was the main bottleneck**. Of 185 titles kept for full text, 139 had no public PDF (Elsevier, MDPI, Wiley, and other OA-labelled venues). Those include many papers that would likely have been eligible. Huang-style failures are logged, not filled in from memory. MDPI’s resolver returned HTTP 403 in a probe; we did not seek paywall copies.

Limits of this write-up: PDF text extraction garbles columns; several leads omit n; two included files are protocols [12,13]; Qiu et al. could not be read because the downloaded file was the wrong article.

## 8. Conclusions

From this 459-record OA export, 44 full texts can be cited. Weekly semaglutide improved time in range as an adjunct to automated insulin delivery in one small type 1 diabetes trial [1]. In people at cardiovascular risk, a 50-trial synthesis associated semaglutide with lower mortality and myocardial infarction and with more gastrointestinal symptoms [14]. Delivery and exosome papers are predominantly preclinical [19–24,43,44]. Nothing was inferred from the 139 titles without a public PDF.
