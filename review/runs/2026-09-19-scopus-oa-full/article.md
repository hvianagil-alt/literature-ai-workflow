# GLP-1 receptor agonists, extra-glycaemic biology, and non-injectable delivery in metabolic disease

## Abstract

Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) and glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) are gut hormones that raise insulin when glucose is high. Licensed analogues of those hormones now treat type 2 diabetes and obesity, yet three practical questions remain open in the papers reviewed here. First, does a weekly GLP-1 receptor agonist (RA) still improve glucose when insulin is already adjusted by an automated pump? In a double-blind crossover of subcutaneous semaglutide versus placebo as adjunct to automated insulin delivery in adults with type 1 diabetes (28 randomized, 24 completers), time in 3.9–10.0 mmol·L⁻¹ rose by 4.8 percentage points (74.2% vs 69.4%) without more time below 3.9 mmol·L⁻¹; HbA1c was 0.5% lower, and two episodes of euglycaemic ketosis without acidosis occurred on semaglutide [1]. Second, how large is the human safety envelope for semaglutide in people already at cardiovascular risk? A 50-trial meta-analysis (54,972 randomized) found lower all-cause mortality (RR 0.85, 95% CI 0.79–0.91) and myocardial infarction (RR 0.77) and more gastrointestinal events [14]. Third, can the peptide be given by mouth, lung, or skin at a fraction of subcutaneous exposure? In rats, pulmonary liraglutide on compact spiky zinc oxide reached about 60% (healthy) and 51% (alloxan-diabetic) of subcutaneous bioavailability [19]; in dogs, an engineered oral GLP-1 RA tablet (MEDI7219) reached about 6% [45]. Those formulation numbers are not human pharmacokinetics, and they cannot be averaged with the clinical trials. Observational time-in-range, tablet bioequivalence, FAERS signals, and utilisation studies answer different questions. Extra-glycaemic work on liver, bone, muscle, heart, retina, brain, and diabetic wounds is mechanistic unless a trial measured the same endpoint in people. Head-to-head human pharmacokinetics of inhaled or microneedle GLP-1 RAs versus subcutaneous injection are not available in this set.

## Keywords

GLP-1 receptor agonists; semaglutide; liraglutide; oral peptide delivery; microneedles; diabetic complications; extracellular vesicles

## 1. Introduction

A reader who does not work on incretin drugs still needs a short map of the physiology, the devices, and the formulation problems that later sections assume. This Introduction is that map. Numbers that appear here are taken from the included full texts; they are not a new experiment.

### 1.1 Native incretin hormones

After a meal, the small intestine releases two peptide hormones that together account for much of the extra insulin secreted when glucose is given by mouth rather than by vein. That extra insulin is the incretin effect. The two hormones are GIP, from K cells, and GLP-1, from L cells [15]. Both are peptides. They bind G-protein-coupled receptors on pancreatic β-cells and raise insulin in a glucose-dependent way, which is why severe hypoglycaemia is uncommon when they are given without insulin. GLP-1 also slows gastric emptying and reduces appetite. Native GLP-1 and GIP are short-lived in blood because dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) clips them and because the kidney clears small peptides [15,45]. Sitagliptin and related DPP-4 inhibitors therefore raise *endogenous* GLP-1 and GIP. They are a different pharmacological class from receptor *agonists*, which are engineered peptides (or, more recently, non-peptide small molecules) that occupy the receptor for hours to days.

Hölscher’s review of Alzheimer and Parkinson disease is useful here even for a metabolic reader, because it states the same receptor biology in another organ. Insulin and insulin-like growth factor-1 signalling is downregulated early in those brains; neuronal glucose uptake falls; astrocytes increase ketone supply; amino acids are burned for energy; synaptic repair and autophagy fail [33]. GLP-1 and GIP receptors exist on neurons and glia. Agonists that were built for diabetes have therefore been tested as candidate disease-modifying drugs for neurodegeneration [33]. That is a hypothesis about extra-glycaemic receptor occupancy, not a claim that every GLP-1 RA trial in diabetes measured cognition.

### 1.2 Licensed analogues and what “weekly” and “oral” actually mean

Once-daily liraglutide, once-weekly semaglutide, and the dual GIP/GLP-1 agonist tirzepatide are the three incretin analogues that Bonga and Padhan discuss as licensed for obesity in people without diabetes [15]. Son’s narrative review of GLP-1-based obesity pharmacology puts the typical weight-loss range at about 10–25% of body weight, with cardiorenal effects that may be partly independent of the kilograms lost, and lists heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis, obstructive sleep apnoea, osteoarthritis, and peripheral vascular disease as conditions now appearing in trial programmes [16]. Those percentages and organ lists are the reviewers’ synthesis of a larger literature. They are not effect sizes recomputed from the primary studies in sections 3–6.

Almost all of those drugs are still injections. Pechenov and colleagues write the reason in plain terms: peptide drugs work, but injection lowers convenience for chronic disease, and oral dosing is blocked by proteases in the gut, poor epithelial permeability, and rapid renal clearance of intact peptide that does reach blood [45]. Marketed oral semaglutide (Rybelsus) is a tablet of the same peptide used in Ozempic, co-formulated with a permeation enhancer (sodium N-(8-[2-hydroxybenzoyl] amino) caprylate, SNAC, in the first-generation product). Nielsen et al. compared a second-generation oral tablet (1.5, 4, and 9 mg) with the first-generation 3, 7, and 14 mg tablets in healthy participants and reported bioequivalence by EMA, FDA, and Japanese PMDA criteria [4]. That study tells a formulator that the new tablet can replace the old one. It does not tell a clinician that oral semaglutide reproduces the time-in-range gain of weekly subcutaneous semaglutide on a closed-loop pump [1].

Orforglipron (LY3502970) is a different chemical class: a non-peptide GLP-1 RA that can be swallowed without SNAC. Ma et al. measured how food changes its pharmacokinetics [5]. Fed dosing lowered geometric mean AUC and Cmax by roughly one-fifth relative to fasting, without changing half-life or time to peak in a way the authors judged clinically meaningful [5]. Small-molecule oral GLP-1 RAs therefore trade peptide-engineering problems for ordinary food-effect problems. They are not interchangeable with SNAC tablets or with pulmonary or microneedle peptides in later sections.

### 1.3 Type 1 diabetes, time in range, and automated insulin delivery

Type 1 diabetes is insulin deficiency from autoimmune β-cell loss. Insulin must be replaced. The most advanced replacement, as Pasqua et al. define it, is automated insulin delivery (AID): a continuous glucose monitor, an insulin pump, and an algorithm that changes the insulin rate from the glucose trace [1]. Consensus CGM targets treat ≥70% of time in 3.9–10.0 mmol·L⁻¹ as corresponding, roughly, to HbA1c ≤7%, the usual threshold used to lower microvascular and macrovascular risk [1]. Large AID trials still left 34–53% of participants above HbA1c 7%, mainly because postprandial glucose is hard to control with insulin alone [1]. AID also does nothing, by itself, about obesity. In the DCCT/EDIC cohort that Pasqua cites, obesity rose from 1% in 1983–1985 to 31% by 2005 [1]. Fragozo-Ramos et al., writing a protocol rather than a results paper, state that only 20–30% of adults with type 1 diabetes reach A1c <7% in usual care, and that randomized evidence for dual GIP/GLP-1 agonists as adjunct to AID is still thin [12].

A GLP-1 RA in this setting is therefore an *adjunct*. It does not replace the pump. The scientific question is whether slowing gastric emptying, cutting glucagon, and reducing appetite still move time in range when the algorithm is already chasing glucose, and whether ketosis appears when carbohydrate intake and insulin both fall. Section 3.1 reports the one double-blind crossover that answers that question in this set [1]. Section 3.2 reports the protocol that will test tirzepatide in the same device setting [12]. Those two papers should not be collapsed: one has outcomes, one does not.

### 1.4 Extra-glycaemic organs: liver, bone, muscle, heart, eye, brain, wounds

GLP-1 receptors are not confined to the islet. That fact is both the source of therapeutic interest and the source of over-reading. Zachou et al. review anti-diabetic drugs in non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD; now often called MASLD). They cite a global prevalence of about 30%, especially in obesity and type 2 diabetes, a spectrum from steatosis to steatohepatitis, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma, and insulin resistance as the shared mechanism that makes diabetes drugs a rational experiment in fatty liver [18]. Whether a GLP-1 RA acts *in the hepatocyte* is a different question from whether weight loss and better insulin action improve liver fat *in vivo*. da Silva Lima et al. applied liraglutide, acyl-GIP, and a dual agonist (MAR709) to human hepatocytes and hepatic stellate cells at concentrations that stimulate insulin release, and saw no change in loaded lipid or in TGF-β-activated stellate-cell behaviour, and no CREB phosphorylation [32]. That cell result sits beside oral semaglutide nanocapsules intended to raise gut GLP-1 and absorbed analogue in MASLD models [23]. The two papers measure different compartments. They are not a contradiction.

Muscle loss on GLP-1 RAs is framed by Mastaitis et al. as an evolved response to caloric restriction: muscle is a large energy consumer, and ActRIIA/B ligands (GDF8/myostatin and activin A) reduce muscle mass; blocking those two ligands spared muscle and increased fat loss in obese male mice and non-human primates treated with a GLP-1 RA [41]. Bone papers in this set put Wnt/β-catenin between semaglutide and osteoblast programmes in ovariectomized rats and in bone-marrow mesenchymal stem cells [36,37]. Cardiac work asks whether GABA_B receptor agonism can blunt GLP-1 RA chronotropy and post-infarct arrhythmia [39]. Shen et al. and Yue et al. take up diabetic retinopathy: prior studies disagree on whether GLP-1 RAs induce it; one paper uses Mendelian randomization plus a systematic review in European-ancestry data [34], the other FAERS plus retinal endothelial assays [35]. Neither is an outcomes trial with adjudicated photocoagulation or vitreous haemorrhage.

Diabetic wounds are a complication of hyperglycaemia and neuropathy, not an incretin endpoint. Chen et al. combined adipose-derived mesenchymal stromal cell (MSC) exosomes with a *Spirulina* hydrogel, with or without laser, in a diabetic rat wound model [44]. There is no GLP-1 payload. The paper is in this review because the original question included stem-cell and vesicle products in metabolic or repair settings, not because exosomes are a GLP-1 formulation.

### 1.5 Why peptides are hard to swallow, inhale, or push through skin

Three physical barriers recur in the delivery papers.

In the gut, proteases clip the peptide, mucus and tight junctions limit uptake, and what is absorbed is often <1% of the dose. Pechenov et al. removed protease sites by amino-acid substitution, bis-lipidated the backbone to promote reversible plasma-protein binding, and used sodium chenodeoxycholate plus propyl gallate in enteric tablets (MEDI7219). Oral-tablet bioavailability was about 6% in dogs, against a 0.4–1% range they cite for many oral peptides [45]. In a dog obesity and insulin-resistance model the same tablets reduced food intake, body weight, and glucose excursions [45]. That is a formulation success for *that molecule in that species*. It is not human SNAC-tablet pharmacokinetics, and it is not marketed oral semaglutide.

In the lung, the problem is getting particles to the distal airway, through mucus, and past macrophages, then across epithelium into blood. Jeong et al. made three zinc-oxide morphologies as inhalable liraglutide carriers and showed that compact spikes (ZO-3, 1–3 μm with ~1.3 μm tips) favoured distal deposition, mucus penetration, and less macrophage uptake; relative bioavailability versus subcutaneous injection was about 60% in healthy rats and 51% in alloxan-diabetic rats [19]. They contrast that with <2% oral bioavailability of marketed oral semaglutide from product literature, not from an oral arm in the same animals [19]. There is no human inhaled liraglutide pharmacokinetic study in this set.

In the skin, dissolving microneedles avoid the gut and the lung. Woo et al. made hyaluronic acid/aminoclay needles carrying a semaglutide–aminoclay nanocomplex; one candidate (Sema-AC/HA10) had a mechanical strength of 0.37 ± 0.020 N/needle, stayed stable at 25 °C, dissolved after insertion, and raised systemic exposure relative to needles loaded with free drug [20]. Yao et al. co-delivered semaglutide and rosiglitazone from an adipose-targeted nanocomposite patch, arguing that GLP-1 withdrawal is followed by weight regain while PPAR-γ agonists are limited by a pro-inflammatory adipose milieu [21]. Zhang et al. loaded GLP-1 RAs into silk-fibroin nanoparticles (encapsulation about 28–33%; about 60% released by day 14) decorated with a type II collagen-binding peptide, then placed them in dissolving microneedles for collagen-induced arthritis in rats [22]. The chassis is similar; the disease, payload targeting, and endpoints are not.

Singh et al. review a related insulin problem: enzymatic degradation and frequent dosing, and PLGA microspheres as a route to sustained or glucose-responsive release [29]. Fredholt et al. co-formulated insulin with sodium caprate (C10) as mini-tablets versus liquid in rats [27]. Yu et al. modified insulin icodec’s acylation side chain [30]. Those papers belong in a peptide-delivery conversation. They do not measure GLP-1 RA time in range.

### 1.6 Public interest and what this review is not

Raubenheimer et al. used Google Trends to relate worldwide search interest in semaglutide to social and print media during off-label weight-loss use and supply shortages [42]. That is infodemiology. It says nothing about efficacy. It does say why utilisation, FAERS, and access papers (section 3.2) matter: the drugs are being used outside the populations that filled the randomized trials.

This paper is a review article in the ordinary journal sense. It does not report a new trial. It summarises included full texts, compares designs, and says where the next measurement should be. Narrative reviews inside the set [15–18,29,33] are treated as maps, not as primary effect sizes. Protocols [12,13] are named as protocols. Animal pharmacokinetics are not human pharmacokinetics.

### 1.7 Aim

This review asks three questions that the included papers can actually speak to. What do recent human studies show for GLP-1 RAs on glycaemia (especially as adjunct to AID), on cardiovascular-risk safety, and on how the drugs are used? What do formulation papers show for non-injectable exposure, and in which species? What extra-glycaemic biology is supported at the level of cells, animals, or people, and what would be a forced “class effect”? The scope is recent work on GLP-1-related agonists, mesenchymal stromal cell or extracellular-vesicle products, and drug-delivery systems in metabolic disease or a closely related inflammatory or tissue-repair setting. Papers that do not share an endpoint are not pooled.

## 2. Methods

Eligible full texts addressed at least one of: a GLP-1 or related incretin agonist; mesenchymal stromal cells or extracellular vesicles as therapy or carrier; or a drug-delivery system, in metabolic disease or a closely related inflammatory or tissue-repair setting. Identification used a Scopus bibliographic export of recent articles. Titles without those terms, or without a metabolic or related setting, were excluded before full-text retrieval. Full texts that could not be retrieved as a public file were not used as evidence. Among files that were read, two were excluded: one inflammatory-bowel delivery paper in which GLP-1 appeared only in passing, and one file whose downloaded PDF was a different article than the bibliographic record. Forty-four full texts from that export were included. One additional full text on oral GLP-1 tablet engineering (MEDI7219) was added to interpret oral peptide bioavailability after the included inhalation paper cited product-literature oral figures [45]. Narrative reviews were kept as background, not as primary effect sizes. Trial protocols were named as protocols. Numbers below are those stated in the papers or their extracted leads; where n or an effect size is missing from the extract, that is stated.

## 3. Clinical incretin use

### 3.1 Glycaemia with insulin and oral products

Pasqua et al. randomized 28 adults with type 1 diabetes in a double-blind crossover of weekly subcutaneous semaglutide (titrated over 11 weeks to 1 mg or the maximum tolerated dose) versus placebo, each followed by 4 weeks of automated insulin delivery (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT05205928) [1]. Twenty-four completed both periods. The primary endpoint was percentage time in 3.9–10.0 mmol·L⁻¹ during those last 4 weeks: 74.2% (SD 9.7) on semaglutide versus 69.4% (SD 10.4) on placebo, mean difference 4.8 (SD 7.6) percentage points (*P* = 0.006) [1]. Time below 3.9 mmol·L⁻¹ (*P* = 0.19) and below 3.0 mmol·L⁻¹ (*P* = 0.65) did not increase; the TIR gain was from less hyperglycaemia [1]. HbA1c fell by 0.5% (IQR −0.7 to −0.2; *P* < 0.001) versus placebo [1]. No diabetic ketoacidosis or severe hypoglycaemia occurred on either intervention; two episodes of recurrent euglycaemic ketosis without acidosis occurred on semaglutide [1]. The trial is small. It is also the only blinded, controlled human glycaemic experiment in this set that holds the insulin-delivery system fixed and changes only the adjunct. It answers whether a GLP-1 RA still helps when insulin is already algorithmically adjusted. It does not establish oral, pulmonary, or microneedle semaglutide as equivalent, and it does not speak to type 2 diabetes cardiovascular outcome trials.

Chen et al. performed a multicentre retrospective study in type 2 diabetes with continuous glucose monitoring, motivated by the gap they note between randomized TIR gains on GLP-1 RAs and a lack of real-world TIR evidence [2]. After 1:1 propensity matching, 202 patients were split equally between GLP-1 RA-based therapy and other oral antidiabetic drugs [2]. At 3–6 months, time in range was 76.0% versus 65.7% (*P* < 0.001), with lower time above range and mean glucose in the GLP-1 RA arm [2]. The direction agrees with Pasqua (higher TIR on a GLP-1 RA). The design does not: mixed molecules, no randomization, residual confounding possible, and a type 2 rather than type 1 population. The two TIR numbers should not be meta-analysed.

Hirotsu et al. switched Japanese adults with type 2 diabetes from DPP-4 inhibitors to oral semaglutide and asked who reached HbA1c <7% (52 mmol·mol⁻¹) [3]. Seventy-four people were enrolled; 44 reached the target (3 mg n=7; 7 mg n=24; 14 mg n=13) and 17 did not [3]. Switching from a DPP-4 inhibitor to an oral GLP-1 RA is a common clinical sequence, because both act on the same hormone axis and there is little reason to continue the inhibitor once an agonist is started. The paper is a dose-finding and predictor pilot. It is not a placebo-controlled efficacy trial.

Nielsen et al. tested bioequivalence of second-generation oral semaglutide tablets (1.5, 4, and 9 mg) against first-generation 3, 7, and 14 mg tablets in healthy participants (NCT05227196) [4]. The design was a randomised, multicentre, open-label, full-replicate crossover at steady state: 20 weeks, four 5-week periods on alternating formulations, with 24-hour sampling at the end of each period [4]. Co-primary endpoints were AUC<sub>0–24h,SS</sub> and Cmax at steady state [4]. Group sizes at recruitment were 222, 201, and 123 for the high, middle, and low dose pairs [4]. EMA, FDA, and PMDA bioequivalence criteria were met at all three dose levels; the safety profile of the second-generation tablet was consistent with the first [4]. This is formulation interchangeability in healthy people, not outcome superiority versus injection in diabetes.

Ma et al. studied oral orforglipron in two phase 1 food-effect crossovers in healthy adults (NCT03929744, NCT05110794) [5]. Study A gave a single 3 mg dose (n=12; mean age 45.0 years; 66.7% male); study B gave multiple 16 mg doses (n=34; mean age 42.8 years; 88.2% male) [5]. Geometric least-squares mean AUC and Cmax were lower by 23.7% and 23.2% in study A, and by 17.6% and 20.9% in study B, in the fed versus fasted states [5]. Half-life and median tmax were comparable; most treatment-emergent events were gastrointestinal; no serious adverse events or deaths were reported [5]. The authors judged the food effect unlikely to produce a meaningful efficacy difference and noted the absence of prandial restrictions as a practical advantage versus SNAC tablets [5]. That is a small-molecule PK claim. It is not a head-to-head with oral semaglutide on HbA1c.

### 3.2 Utilisation, safety signals, access, and protocols

Swift et al. described real-world lines of therapy, duration of each line, and reasons for ending a line after initiation of oral semaglutide in type 2 diabetes [6]. Cohort n is not stated in the extracted lead [6]. The paper’s job is sequencing, not efficacy.

Xiong et al. mined FAERS (2019–2023) for adverse-event reports after oral semaglutide tablets, noting that earlier GLP-1 RAs were injection-only and that tablet safety in the wild still needed watching [7]. Spontaneous-report disproportionality is not incidence in a defined denominator. Signals from FAERS can generate hypotheses; they cannot replace Sillassen’s trial-based relative risks [14].

Abdallah et al. evaluated GLP-1 RAs for insufficient weight loss or weight regain after bariatric surgery, which remains the most effective treatment for severe obesity but fails a fraction of patients [8]. The extracted lead states efficacy and safety as aims, without n or an effect size [8]. Post-bariatric use is a distinct clinical question from AID adjunct or from primary obesity pharmacotherapy in Bonga and Son [15,16].

Wang et al. examined racial and ethnic differences in SGLT2 inhibitor and GLP-1 RA uptake among US Medicare beneficiaries with type 2 diabetes plus heart failure, atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, or chronic kidney disease in 2013–2019 [9]. Ali et al. modelled introduction of SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 RAs in low- and middle-income countries, asking what would happen to insulin dose and complications, because those drugs’ effects on insulin requirement in LMIC health systems are not well described [10]. Both papers treat access and substitution as part of effectiveness. They should not be pooled with Pasqua’s TIR difference [1] or Chen’s matched CGM contrast [2].

Lunati et al. reported two young women with latent autoimmune diabetes of youth (onset 8–29 years, slow progression toward insulin dependence) treated with oral semaglutide plus basal insulin [11]. One was a 20-year-old with BMI 31.8 kg·m⁻², multiple autoantibodies, and residual C-peptide, presenting without ketoacidosis [11]. Residual insulin secretion is a plausible substrate for an incretin agonist. Two cases cannot estimate a class effect in LADA/LADY.

Fragozo-Ramos et al. published the AID-JUNCT protocol: a prospective, randomised, parallel-group, open-label superiority trial of tirzepatide added to AID versus standard of care in adults with type 1 diabetes [12]. Planned enrolment is 42 people aged 18–65 years, T1D for at least 12 months, AID for at least three months, A1C 6.5–10%, BMI ≥23 kg·m⁻², randomised 1:1 to tirzepatide (target 5.0 mg after titration) or standard of care for 16 weeks [12]. The primary endpoint is CGM time in 3.9–10.0 mmol·L⁻¹ [12]. Until that trial reports, dual-agonist adjunct to AID is an unanswered clinical question. Pasqua used semaglutide, not tirzepatide, and a blinded crossover rather than open-label parallel groups [1]. The two designs will not be interchangeable even after AID-JUNCT completes.

Tian, Zhang, Zhang et al. published a protocol of transcutaneous auricular vagus-nerve stimulation plus semaglutide in type 2 diabetes, aimed at efficacy and at gastrointestinal adverse effects that limit GLP-1 RA titration [13]. The file contains no outcome data [13]. Device-based mitigation of nausea is a reasonable trial idea; it is not evidence that taVNS changes semaglutide’s effect size.

### 3.3 Cardiovascular-risk safety and narrative clinical reviews

Sillassen et al. searched six electronic databases and other sources to 31 March 2025 and meta-analysed randomized placebo-controlled trials of oral or subcutaneous semaglutide in people at increased cardiovascular risk, with Trial Sequential Analysis, Cochrane RoB 2, an eight-step threshold procedure, and GRADE [14]. Fifty trials (54,972 randomized; 30,281 semaglutide, 24,691 placebo) contributed [14]. Nineteen trials (38%) enrolled people with type 2 diabetes, 17 (34%) with overweight, 5 (10%) with type 2 diabetes and chronic kidney disease, 5 (10%) with type 2 diabetes and overweight, and 4 (8%) other populations [14]. All-cause mortality RR was 0.85 (95% CI 0.79–0.91; *I*² = 0%), myocardial infarction RR 0.77 (0.69–0.85), and serious adverse events RR 0.93 (0.88–0.98) [14]. Non-serious gastrointestinal events were more common: nausea RR 3.00 (2.63–3.42), vomiting RR 4.12 (3.47–4.90), diarrhoea RR 1.88 (1.68–2.11) [14]. This is the largest human safety synthesis in the set. It is restricted to people already at cardiovascular risk and to semaglutide versus placebo. It is not a mortality estimate for inhaled zinc-oxide liraglutide [19], silk-fibroin joint microneedles [22], or MSC-exosome hydrogels [44].

Bonga and Padhan review liraglutide, semaglutide, and tirzepatide for weight reduction in obesity without diabetes, including cost-effectiveness comments and a reminder that lifestyle change remains the background of pharmacotherapy [15]. Son reviews GLP-1-based obesity pharmacology and states that liraglutide, semaglutide, and tirzepatide can produce about 10–25% weight loss with cardiorenal claims that may be partly weight-independent [16]. Wallentowitz et al. review incretin-based therapy in maternal obesity and metabolic syndrome [17]. Pregnancy integrates insulin signalling, lipid metabolism, placental endocrine function, mitochondrial remodelling, and inflammation; maternal obesity and gestational weight gain are risk factors for childhood overweight [17]. The review discusses how GLP-1 and dual GIP/GLP-1 agonists might interact with those adaptations and with long-term offspring risk [17]. It does not report a pregnancy RCT in the extracted lead [17]. Zachou et al. review anti-diabetic drugs in NAFLD as summarised in section 1.4 [18]. None of [15–18] should be treated as new primary effect sizes.

## 4. Non-injectable and locally targeted delivery

### 4.1 Lung, skin, and joint

Jeong et al. engineered three zinc-oxide morphologies as inhalable liraglutide carriers: smooth spheres ZO-1 (5–7 μm), spiky urchin-like ZO-2 (5–8 μm with ~3.5 μm tips), and compact spikes ZO-3 (1–3 μm with ~1.3 μm tips) [19]. Particle-image velocimetry showed morphology-dependent aerodynamics; ZO-3 favoured turbulence-driven distal deposition, mucus penetration, and less macrophage uptake, so that contact with epithelium lasted longer [19]. After intratracheal LG@ZO-3, relative bioavailability versus subcutaneous injection was about 60% in healthy rats and 51% in alloxan-diabetic rats [19]. The authors contrast that with <2% oral bioavailability of marketed oral semaglutide from product literature, not from an oral arm in the same animals [19]. The morphology result is the scientific contribution: spike geometry changed where particles went and which cells ate them. The oral-semaglutide contrast is a literature number, not a within-study oral arm. There is no human inhaled liraglutide PK in this set.

Woo et al. made dissolving hyaluronic acid/aminoclay microneedles carrying a semaglutide–aminoclay nanocomplex (Sema-AC) formed by electrostatic interaction, then mixed with hyaluronic acid of several molecular weights and cast in reverse PDMS moulds [20]. Among candidates, Sema-AC/HA10 had a mechanical strength of 0.37 ± 0.020 N/needle, retained physicochemical and morphological properties at 25 °C, kept peptide conformation, and dissolved after skin insertion [20]. Needles containing the nanocomplex raised systemic drug exposure compared with needles loaded with free drug, and the authors report reductions in glucose, HbA1c, lipids, intake, and body weight in rats [20]. Group sizes are not stated in the extracted abstract [20]. The paper is a non-injectable obesity formulation study in animals. It is not a human bioequivalence trial.

Yao et al. built an adipose-targeted nanocomposite microneedle (LRSG-SG@MN) co-delivering semaglutide and rosiglitazone [21]. The stated rationale is that GLP-1 withdrawal is followed by weight regain, while PPAR-γ agonists are limited by a pro-inflammatory adipose milieu; lipid droplets target adipocytes for PPAR-γ-driven mitochondrial biogenesis while semaglutide is intended to shift macrophages from M1 to M2 [21]. Quantitative animal outcomes are not stated in the extracted lead [21]. The construct is immuno-metabolic local delivery, not a systemic TIR study.

Zhang et al. loaded GLP-1 RAs into silk-fibroin nanoparticles (encapsulation about 28–33%; about 60% released by day 14) decorated with the type II collagen-binding peptide WYRGRL, then placed them in dissolving microneedles for collagen-induced arthritis in rats [22]. They argue GLP-1 RAs suppress chondrocyte ferroptosis via cGAS–STING and induce M2 polarisation, but articular exposure after systemic dosing is poor [22]. That is a rheumatology delivery paper. Pooling it with Woo’s obesity needles or Jeong’s lung particles as one “microneedle or inhalation effect” would mix joints, fat, and alveoli.

### 4.2 Oral nanocarriers, permeation enhancers, and peptide engineering

Domingues et al. gave oral semaglutide-loaded lipid nanocapsules intended both to raise native GLP-1 secretion and to absorb encapsulated analogue in MASLD models, aiming for a larger metabolic and histologic effect than marketed oral semaglutide [23]. The formulation is a bet that local gut and absorbed analogue together can do what da Silva Lima’s hepatocyte cultures did not do [32]. That is a delivery hypothesis, not a finding that the two papers disagree on the same assay.

Sandmeier et al. compared reverse micelles (ethyl lauroyl arginate or docusate) versus hydrophobic ion pairs (semaglutide–ELA) for loading semaglutide into self-emulsifying systems; droplet sizes were 50–300 nm, with Caco-2 permeability and cytocompatibility readouts [24]. Kweon et al. designed a liraglutide nanoformulation for ASBT- and EGFR-mediated intestinal absorption, coordinating two epithelial uptake paths rather than relying on unspecific leak [25]. Dangi and Sharma formulated modified-release semaglutide (FPR-8) to flatten a wide plasma-concentration range quoted as 5.85–38.14 for unformulated solution (units not restated in the lead) over 24 hours in animal PK [26]. These four papers attack gut barriers from four directions: lipid nanocapsules, self-emulsifying reverse micelles versus ion pairs, transporter-targeted nanoparticles, and modified release. None reports a human therapeutic bioavailability in the extracts used here.

Pechenov et al. combined protease-site substitution and bis-lipidation with sodium chenodeoxycholate/propyl gallate in enteric tablets (MEDI7219), as already outlined in section 1.5 [45]. Oral-tablet bioavailability was about 6% in dogs, versus a 0.4–1% range they cite for many oral peptides [45]. In a dog obesity and insulin-resistance model the tablets reduced food intake, body weight, and glucose excursions; exact n and the numeric PD endpoints are not restated in the extracted lead [45]. MEDI7219 is not marketed oral semaglutide. Dog bioavailability is not human SNAC-tablet PK. The paper is here because Jeong’s oral <2% figure needed an independent oral-peptide bioavailability full text, not because MEDI7219 can be substituted into Pasqua’s AID protocol.

Fredholt et al. co-formulated insulin with a fixed 26 mg·kg⁻¹ sodium caprate (C10) as mini-tablets versus liquid after intestinal administration in rats [27]. Absorption was dose-dependent; a 75 IU·kg⁻¹ insulin mini-tablet with C10 increased bioavailability 26-fold, with transient mucosal erosion that was similar for solid and liquid C10 [27]. The payload is insulin, not a GLP-1 RA. The permeation-enhancer lesson (solid C10 can match liquid C10, at a mucosal cost) is still relevant to oral peptide tablets.

Klotsman et al. implanted OKV-119, configured to release exenatide for 84 days, in five purpose-bred cats and followed them for 112 days [28]. Plasma exenatide was detectable at day 7 and remained above baseline beyond day 84; caloric intake and body weight fell over the first 28 days in four of five cats [28]. That is veterinary implant pharmacokinetics, not human obesity-trial evidence.

Singh et al. review PLGA microspheres as a route to sustained, possibly glucose-responsive insulin, citing enzymatic degradation and frequent dosing of current insulin as the problem [29]. Yu et al. modified the acylation side chain of insulin icodec and report TBE001-A-S033 as a weekly candidate tested in ICR and db/db mice, where it prolonged glucose control relative to the design goal of matching weekly icodec [30]. Xu et al. built an *E. coli* biosynthetic platform to insert D-amino acids and ornithines into tirzepatide, because industrial solid-phase synthesis can place non-canonical residues but scales poorly for mutant libraries aimed at oral stability [31]. [27–31] are peptide-delivery or analog-engineering papers in a metabolic setting. They are not clinical GLP-1 RA outcome trials.

## 5. Extra-glycaemic biology and public interest

da Silva Lima et al. applied liraglutide, acyl-GIP, or the dual agonist MAR709 to human hepatocyte and hepatic stellate cell lines and primary cells, at concentrations that stimulate insulin release [32]. None of the three compounds changed hepatocyte lipid after oleic/palmitic acid loading, or TGF-β-activated HSC behaviour, or CREB phosphorylation in those cells [32]. The authors conclude that beneficial effects in people with MASH are likely indirect (body weight, insulin resistance, glycaemia) rather than a direct hepatocyte or stellate-cell action [32]. That reading is specific to the concentrations and cell types tested. It does not rule out a higher local analogue concentration from a delivery system [23], and it does not rule out gut-derived GLP-1 acting on other cell types.

Hölscher reviews early downregulation of insulin/IGF-1 signalling and reduced neuronal glucose utilisation in Alzheimer and Parkinson disease, compensatory ketone and amino-acid use, failing synaptic repair, chronic inflammation, and the case for repurposed GLP-1 RAs and dual GLP-1/GIP agonists as candidate disease-modifying therapies, citing animal studies and clinical trials in that literature [33]. This review does not re-analyse those CNS trials.

Shen et al. combined Mendelian randomization with a systematic review of GLP-1 RAs and diabetic retinopathy in European-ancestry data, noting that prior studies disagree on whether GLP-1 RAs induce retinopathy [34]. Yue et al. analysed FAERS for semaglutide and diabetic retinopathy and added assays in human retinal microvascular endothelial cells [35]. Neither [34] nor [35] replaces a dedicated retinopathy outcomes trial. Rapid glucose lowering itself can worsen retinopathy in the short term in other diabetes drug classes; whether that mechanism, a direct retinal GLP-1 effect, or reporting bias explains FAERS signals is not settled in these two papers.

Abo-Elenin et al. studied semaglutide in ovariectomized rats as a postmenopausal osteoporosis model [36]. The clinical problem they name is the imbalance of resorption and formation after estrogen loss, often with weight gain [36]. They link GLP-1 RA signals in adipose and mesenchymal stem cells to β-catenin-dependent osteoprotection [36]. Tian, Liu, Bao, and Li reported that semaglutide increased BMSC proliferation (CCK-8, flow cytometry), ALP activity, mineralised nodules, migration, and OCN/RUNX2 mRNA and protein via Wnt/LRP5/β-catenin [37]. Animal n is not stated in that extract [37]. Both papers put the same pathway (Wnt/β-catenin) between semaglutide and bone-forming cells. They are preclinical.

Sun et al. gave semaglutide 100 μg·kg⁻¹ to high-fat-diet C57BL/6J mice (ND / HFD / SHF) and transferred faeces (MT) or bacteria (FMT) from treated mice into antibiotic-pretreated recipients, asking whether microbiota carry the metabolic benefit [38]. That is a transfer experiment. If the recipients improve, microbiota are sufficient for some of the phenotype in that model; it still does not identify a human strain or a clinical probiotic.

Qi et al. report that GABA_B receptor agonism blunts GLP-1 RA chronotropy and reduces post-infarct arrhythmogenesis in a preclinical model [39]. Resting heart-rate rise is a known GLP-1 RA effect in people; whether GABA_B agonism is a usable clinical countermeasure is not answered by the animal paper.

Yang et al. report that electroacupuncture raised semaglutide exposure and reduced β-cell apoptosis in a type 2 diabetes model, with dose-sparing in the title; numerical PK values are not stated in the extracted lead [40]. Gastrointestinal adverse effects that limit titration are the clinical problem the authors want to work around [40]. The extract does not give the concentration numbers needed to judge the size of the sparing.

Mastaitis et al. show that GLP-1 RA–driven caloric restriction can cost muscle because muscle is a major energy consumer; GDF8 and activin A are the two main ActRIIA/B ligands that reduce muscle; dual blockade prevented muscle loss associated with GLP-1 RAs and even increased muscle mass in obese male mice and non-human primates, while fat loss increased [41]. The quality-of-weight-loss question is clinical; the evidence here is mouse and primate. No human body-composition RCT of this combination is in the set.

Raubenheimer et al. used Google Trends to relate worldwide search interest in semaglutide to social and print media during off-label weight-loss use and supply shortages [42]. That is infodemiology, not a treatment effect. It does explain why FAERS and utilisation papers exist: demand outran the labelled populations.

Khater et al. loaded *Phyllanthus niruri* into niosomes (225.1 ± 19.6 nm; encapsulation 73.5 ± 4.2%) and compared seven rat groups: control, PHYLN-NIO alone, high-fat diet, HFD + semaglutide, HFD + semaglutide + exendin 9–39, HFD + PHYLN-NIO, and HFD + PHYLN-NIO + exendin 9–39 (n = 8/group in the methods of that paper) [43]. PHYLN-NIO reduced weight, BMI, steatosis, and inflammation; benefit tracked MALAT1/miR-206/GLP-1R signalling and was reversed by the GLP-1R antagonist [43]. Botanical niosomes that signal through GLP-1R are not a licensed GLP-1 RA. The antagonist reversal is useful receptor biology. It does not license substitution for semaglutide tablets.

## 6. Mesenchymal stromal cell exosomes in diabetic wounds

Chen et al. combined adipose-derived MSC exosomes with a *Spirulina* hydrogel that incorporates chlorophyll derivatives (AD-MSC-exo@SP), with or without laser, in a diabetic rat wound model [44]. Transcriptome sequencing of wound tissue showed fewer endothelial cells and M2 macrophages in the untreated diabetic wound; the hydrogel was described as inhibiting biofilm, shifting M2/angiogenic programmes, and accelerating closure, with the laser combination strongest, including higher proliferative and angiogenic proteins and less inflammation in the animal experiments [44]. Group sizes are not stated in the extracted text [44]. There is no GLP-1 payload. Ageing and diabetes make chronic wounds a clinical problem; current dressings often fail when infection and poor perfusion coincide [44]. This is a vesicle-plus-device strategy for a diabetic complication. Combining it with Jeong’s lung particles or Pasqua’s AID trial would conflate a wound-healing construct with incretin pharmacology.

## 7. Discussion

The clinical picture that can be stated with confidence is narrow and design-specific. Weekly subcutaneous semaglutide improved time in range as an adjunct to automated insulin delivery in one small, carefully blinded type 1 diabetes crossover, by cutting hyperglycaemia rather than by trading it for hypoglycaemia, at the cost of gastrointestinal burden during titration and rare euglycaemic ketosis [1]. That result answers a practical question: whether a GLP-1 RA still helps when insulin is already algorithmically adjusted. The 4.8 percentage-point TIR gain moved mean TIR from just under the 70% consensus target to just over it (69.4% to 74.2%) [1]. In a larger, longer, or less selected AID population the same adjunct might or might not cross that threshold. Two ketosis episodes without acidosis are a reminder that carbohydrate restriction plus less insulin can raise ketones even when glucose looks acceptable [1]. The trial does not establish oral, pulmonary, or microneedle semaglutide as equivalent, and it does not speak to type 2 diabetes CVOTs.

The matched CGM study in type 2 diabetes moves in the same direction (higher TIR on GLP-1 RA-based therapy) under a weaker design [2]. A 10.3 percentage-point observational TIR gap after matching is larger than Pasqua’s randomized 4.8 points, which is what one expects when confounding and mixed drugs remain [2]. Oral semaglutide after DPP-4 inhibitors reached HbA1c <7% in a subset of a Japanese pilot, which is useful for titration practice (most successes sat at 7 mg) and uninformative as a pooled efficacy estimate [3]. Bioequivalence of two oral semaglutide tablet generations in hundreds of healthy participants, by EMA, FDA, and PMDA criteria [4], and a food effect of about 18–24% on orforglipron exposure in two small phase 1 studies [5], are formulation facts. They tell us the tablet can be swapped or that the small molecule need not be tied to a fasting ritual. They do not tell us that oral products reproduce Pasqua’s AID result.

Sillassen et al. supply the only large, pooled human safety and mortality estimates here [14]. Lower all-cause mortality and myocardial infarction with more nausea, vomiting, and diarrhoea is coherent with GLP-1 RA trials as a class. The *I*² of 0% for mortality is unusually clean and should be read with the restriction in mind: people already at cardiovascular risk, semaglutide versus placebo, mixed oral and subcutaneous, mixed diabetes and overweight [14]. Trial Sequential Analysis and GRADE are strengths of that paper’s methods. The synthesis still cannot be borrowed as a safety envelope for zinc-oxide inhalation [19], silk-fibroin joint microneedles [22], or MSC-exosome hydrogels [44]. FAERS work on oral tablets [7] and on retinopathy [35], together with Mendelian randomization of retinopathy [34], flags signals. Rapid improvement in glucose can worsen retinopathy in other settings; these papers do not settle mechanism. Utilisation [6,9], post-bariatric use [8], and LMIC microsimulation [10] show that access and sequencing are part of effectiveness in the wild. They should not be meta-analysed with TIR or mortality.

Narrative reviews of obesity pharmacotherapy [15,16], pregnancy [17], and NAFLD [18] and Hölscher’s CNS review [33] are maps of a larger literature. Treating their stated 10–25% weight-loss range [16] or 30% NAFLD prevalence [18] as results of the primary studies above would double-count. Pregnancy remains a hole: Wallentowitz can only reason from metabolic physiology and from labelled restrictions, because this set has no pregnancy RCT [17].

Formulation science in this set is a scatter of routes, not a pipeline with one readout. Pulmonary liraglutide on compact spiky zinc oxide reached roughly half to three-fifths of subcutaneous exposure in rats, with morphology controlling aerodynamics and macrophage clearance [19]. That is a serious inhalation-biology result. It is not human lung PK. The <2% oral-semaglutide contrast is product literature rather than a within-study oral arm [19]. Pechenov’s ~6% dog bioavailability for MEDI7219, after peptide engineering plus bile-salt/propyl gallate tablets, shows that oral peptide BA can be moved out of the sub-1% band for a different molecule in a different species, with food-intake and glucose-excursion changes in a dog obesity model [45]. Microneedles for semaglutide [20], adipose-targeted semaglutide/rosiglitazone [21], and articular GLP-1 RA nanoparticles [22] share a dissolving-patch chassis and almost nothing else: obesity glycaemia, immuno-metabolic fat browning, and RA cartilage/ferroptosis. Woo at least reports a needle force (0.37 N) and a PK advantage of the nanocomplex over free drug in the patch [20]. Yao and Zhang do not give n in the extracts used here [21,22]. Oral LNCs for MASLD [23], SEDDS reverse micelles versus ion pairs [24], ASBT/EGFR liraglutide nanoparticles [25], and modified-release FPR-8 [26] are complementary attacks on gut barriers; none reports a human therapeutic BA in the extracts used here. Insulin–C10 mini-tablets raised bioavailability 26-fold in rats at the cost of transient mucosal erosion that was similar for solid and liquid enhancer [27]. Weekly insulin analogs [30], PLGA insulin microspheres [29], feline exenatide implants with measurable plasma drug past day 84 in five cats [28], and biosynthetic tirzepatide mutants [31] belong in the same peptide-delivery conversation and must not be averaged into a “GLP-1 delivery effect.”

Extra-glycaemic biology is similarly non-fungible. Direct incretin agonism failed to clear lipid from human hepatocytes or to quiet HSCs at insulin-secretory concentrations, and failed to phosphorylate CREB there [32], which argues that MASLD benefit, if it occurs clinically, is more likely indirect (weight, insulin, gut GLP-1) unless a delivery system raises local analogue exposure, as Domingues intend with oral LNCs [23]. Those two papers are not in contradiction; they measure different compartments. Bone papers implicate Wnt/β-catenin in ovariectomized rats and BMSCs [36,37]. Muscle loss on GLP-1 RAs is framed as an evolved response to caloric restriction, mitigated by GDF8/activin A blockade in mice and non-human primates [41]. If that combination is tested in people, body composition and function (not scale weight alone) should be the endpoints. Cardiac work links GABA_B agonism to less GLP-1 RA-associated chronotropy and arrhythmia after infarct [39]. Microbiota transfer from semaglutide-treated obese mice [38] and electroacupuncture-related exposure/apoptosis claims [40] are mechanistic hypotheses. Khater’s niosomes act through GLP-1R in high-fat-diet rats and reverse with exendin 9–39, with eight animals per group [43]; that supports receptor biology, not interchangeability with semaglutide tablets. Chen’s AD-MSC exosome–*Spirulina* hydrogel with laser is a wound-healing construct whose transcriptome points to endothelium and M2 macrophages [44]. Combining it with Jeong or Pasqua would conflate a diabetic complication therapy with incretin pharmacology.

Two included files are still protocols [12,13]. AID-JUNCT will test tirzepatide 5 mg versus standard of care, open-label, in 42 AID users over 16 weeks, with TIR as primary endpoint [12]. That is a different agonist, a different control, and a different blinding status from Pasqua [1]. The taVNS–semaglutide protocol asks whether a device can spare gastrointestinal adverse effects [13]. Until both report, dual-agonist adjunct to automated delivery and device-based mitigation of GLP-1 RA gastrointestinal effects remain unanswered. Lunati’s two LADY cases [11] illustrate residual C-peptide as a plausible substrate for oral semaglutide plus basal insulin; they are not a trial.

Limitations of the *evidence* are these. Human comparative glycaemic data that can be described with a primary endpoint, a control, and a *P* value are essentially Pasqua [1] plus a matched observational TIR study [2]. Delivery and vesicle papers are animal or in vitro. Several extracts omit n. Endpoints do not overlap enough for a numeric synthesis across sections 3–6. Head-to-head human PK of pulmonary or microneedle GLP-1 RAs versus subcutaneous injection, a second randomized type 1 AID trial with outcomes, a pregnancy RCT, and a human body-composition trial of myostatin/activin blockade plus a GLP-1 RA are not in this literature as read.

What should be measured next follows from those holes, not from a wish list. A second AID adjunct trial should keep CGM TIR as primary, add ketone surveillance, and, if dual agonists are used, say so rather than pooling with semaglutide [12]. Any inhaled or microneedle GLP-1 RA that claims to compete with injection needs human relative bioavailability versus subcutaneous dosing in the same volunteers, with particle morphology or needle force stated as in Jeong and Woo [19,20]. Oral peptide programmes should report species, enhancer, and percent BA in the same table, and should not equate dog MEDI7219 with human SNAC semaglutide [45,4]. MASLD programmes should distinguish weight-mediated change from a direct hepatic effect, given da Silva Lima [32]. Muscle-sparing combinations should pre-specify lean mass and function [41]. Wound-vesicle studies should state group size and should not be indexed as incretin trials [44].

## 8. Conclusions

Weekly semaglutide can improve time in range when added to automated insulin delivery in type 1 diabetes, by reducing hyperglycaemia, in a small randomized crossover [1]. In people at cardiovascular risk, pooled randomized evidence associates semaglutide with lower mortality and myocardial infarction and with more gastrointestinal symptoms [14]. Real-world TIR, oral-tablet PK, FAERS, and utilisation studies refine practice and access; they do not replace those two anchors [2–10]. Non-injectable GLP-1 RA delivery is scientifically active—morphology-tuned pulmonary carriers [19], microneedles [20–22], oral nanocarriers [23–26], and engineered oral tablets with measurable dog bioavailability [45]—but remains preclinical and route-specific. Extra-glycaemic findings argue against a simple direct hepatic incretin effect [32], for Wnt-linked bone actions [36,37], and for muscle-sparing when myostatin/activin A is blocked during GLP-1 treatment [41]. Exosome hydrogels address diabetic wounds by a different biology [44]. The field’s next clinical measurements that would change practice are human PK of non-injectable GLP-1 RAs versus subcutaneous dosing, and completed adjunct trials in automated insulin delivery [12].
