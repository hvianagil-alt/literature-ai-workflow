# Non-thermal food decontamination techniques and their impact on various food matrices

- **Citation (as given in the paper / filename):** Weihe T, Nestel S, Spilker U, Ehlbeck J, Schnabel U. 2026. J. Phys. D: Appl. Phys. 59:323004. doi:10.1088/1361-6463/ae8e87
- **Source file:** papers/2026-09-20-scopus-hpp/extracted/Weihe2026.txt
- **Extracted:** 2026-09-20

## Research question

How do non-thermal decontamination methods (UV, electron-beam irradiation, atmospheric-pressure plasma, ultrasound, HPP, supercritical fluids, PEF, protective cultures, membrane filtration) inactivate microbes in liquid, semi-solid, and solid foods, and how do they change organoleptic and quality traits?

## Methods

Open-access topical / critical narrative review. The authors summarize technical requirements and quality impacts by matrix class (liquid vs semi-solid/solid), with a comparison table of mechanism, penetration, typical log reductions, quality effects, regulation, and NASA-style technology readiness level (TRL). Supplementary Table S1 is cited for food-category mapping. No systematic search protocol, PRISMA flow, or meta-analysis is described.

## Sample / data

No primary dataset. Cited experimental examples include UV-C absorption coefficients for liquids (e.g. distilled water 0.007–0.01 cm⁻¹ vs milk 300 cm⁻¹ at 254 nm); UV pulsed-light cheese doses (4.2 or 8.4 J cm⁻²); HPP typical 100–600 MPa for seconds to 30 min; historical Hite milk treatment 650 MPa / 10 min. Number of included studies is not reported.

## Claim-ready facts

- **Design:** Open-access topical / critical narrative review (no PRISMA N)
- **Population / model:** Liquid, semi-solid, and solid human food matrices (juices, milk, cheese, meat, seafood, produce, packaged RTE)
- **n:** not reported (narrative review; no count of included studies)
- **Intervention / comparator:** UV, EBI, APP, US, HPP, SCF, PEF, membrane filtration, protective cultures vs conventional heat and vs untreated references in the cited studies
- **Primary endpoint:** Matrix-dependent microbial inactivation (log10 CFU) plus organoleptic/quality change (color, texture, odor, sensory panels)
- **Primary result:** Authors’ synthesis: no single method is universal; reported bactericidal effects reach up to ~7 log10 in some studies. HPP typical reported efficacy 3–6 log10 against vegetative cells with uniform bulk treatment, TRL 9, but limited sporicidal activity unless combined with heat; UV-C TRL 9 for water and 7–8 for juices with RF 3–5 log10 on surfaces/clear liquids. HPP-treated juices/smoothies are generally closer to fresh than thermally treated products in the cited sensory work.
- **Cannot show:** Cannot rank technologies in a pooled effect-size, prove commercial superiority, or claim spore-safe HPP without heat; cannot supply a new experimental log-reduction for a defined product.

## Key findings

- UV-C (~254 nm) is limited by turbidity/opacity (milk A ≈ 300 cm⁻¹); opaque liquids need thin-film presentation. Rossitto et al. flavor differences in milk at 880 and 1760 J L⁻¹ attributed to lipid oxidation. (UV sanitation section; Table 1)
- HPP typically 100–600 MPa, hold from seconds to 30 min, packaged in water-filled vessels; ineffective against bacterial spores and not applicable to dry foods. (HPP section)
- Passion-fruit juice sensory: after HPP, only color appearance varied significantly vs untreated; aroma, sweetness, astringency, and mouthfeel did not. Strawberry juice color was not adversely affected, unlike strawberry puree with residual enzymes. (Liquid foods / HPP)
- Orange juice HPP retained ascorbic acid and fresh flavor vs heat; color change correlated linearly with ascorbic acid loss. Mango juice 200–400 MPa weakened pithy rosin smell (pressure-dependent aroma-active compound decay). (HPP liquid foods)
- Dairy enzymes: lipoprotein lipase, xanthine oxidase, lactoperoxidase resistant up to 400 MPa; phosphohexose isomerase, γ-glutamyltransferase, and alkaline phosphatase partially inactivated above 350, 400, and 600 MPa and completely at 550, 630, and 800 MPa, respectively. (HPP dairy)
- Clariana et al.: 600 MPa on sliced vacuum dry-cured ham — no change in fatty acid content, protein, or antioxidant enzyme activity; lipases/phospholipases still active over 50 d storage. (HPP solids)
- Table 4: HPP mechanism = hydrostatic disruption of membranes/proteins; bulk uniform; RF 3–6 log10 vegetative cells; commercially established for juices, RTE, guacamole, seafood. (Table 4)
- Conclusion: methods are technology- and matrix-dependent; HPP can produce off-odors; APP/US can alter texture/color; SCF can extract desirable components; PEF may displace proteins electrophoretically. (Conclusion)

## Limitations (as stated by the authors, or evident from the methods)

- High capital cost for some methods (HPP, some ultrasound); bactericidal potential must be re-verified by matrix.
- HPP does not inactivate spores; dry and some dense/frozen products are poor candidates.
- Knowledge gaps: quantitative RONS in plasma treatments, spore strategies, matrix-specific sensory/nutrition, molecular explanations of quality change, pilot-scale cost–benefit and standardized protocols.
- Narrative review without a reported search N or risk-of-bias assessment.

## Relevance to our research question

Directly on-scope: compares HPP with other nonthermal decontamination tools and hurdle-style combinations, with explicit limits (spores, dry foods, off-flavor) needed for a HPP/hurdle synthesis.
