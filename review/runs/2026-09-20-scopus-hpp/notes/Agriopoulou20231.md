# Recent Advances in High Pressure Processing of Milk and Milk Products - A review

- **Citation (as given in the paper / filename):** Agriopoulou S, Severoğlu M, Varzakas T, Özer HB (2023). Journal of Agricultural Sciences (Tarım Bilimleri Dergisi), 29(1): 1–12. DOI: 10.15832/ankutbd.1150112
- **Source file:** papers/2026-09-20-scopus-hpp/Agriopoulou20231.pdf
- **Extracted:** 2026-09-20

## Research question
How does high hydrostatic pressure (HPP) change microbial safety, milk-fat and protein structure, and quality of milk and milk products relative to thermal processing?

## Methods
Narrative review of HPP dairy literature (technological background, microbial inactivation, fat/protein effects, cheese and other products). No PRISMA counts.

## Sample / data
Cited experimental studies on cow, goat, donkey, and human milk and dairy products; Table 1 compiles example HPP conditions and log reductions. Number of included studies not reported.

## Claim-ready facts
- **Design:** Narrative review.
- **Population / model:** Milk and milk products (cow, goat, donkey, human milk among cited work).
- **n:** not reported as a study count.
- **Intervention / comparator:** HPP typically 400–600 MPa versus thermal pasteurisation/UHT as discussed in the introduction.
- **Primary endpoint:** Microbial inactivation (pathogens and spoilage), casein micelle and fat-globule changes, immunoglobulin retention.
- **Primary result:** The authors state that 400–600 MPa is in practice sufficient for microbial control depending on product structure and load; Table 1 lists examples such as raw milk 600 MPa / 5 min / 18 °C giving 5 log reductions for E. coli, Salmonella, and L. monocytogenes (as compiled from cited work). Spores are more resistant than vegetative cells, yeasts and moulds.
- **Cannot show:** A pooled D-value or a single industrial milk process equivalent to legal thermal pasteurisation; toxin inactivation is flagged as not guaranteed even when cells die.

## Key findings
- Vegetative milk-associated pathogens (STEC, Salmonella, Listeria) are discussed as HPP-sensitive in the 400–600 MPa range; spores and some Gram-positive organisms are more resistant (microbiology section).
- HPP at 200 MPa for 5–10 min failed to inactivate E. coli O157:H7 and M. tuberculosis in a cited Özer et al. 2022 project; 400 or 600 MPa did inactivate the target pathogens (same paragraph).
- Multi-cycle 2 × 2.5 min at 600 MPa was cited as giving more microbial kill with better quality than 1 × 5 min at 600 MPa (Yang et al. 2020 as cited).
- Pressures above 400 MPa break casein micelles; fat globules in cow’s milk were reported unchanged at 100–600 MPa / 20 °C / 60 min in one cited study.

## Limitations (as stated by the authors, or evident from the methods)
- Narrative, not systematic; heterogeneous cited matrices.
- Toxins may survive HPP even if cells do not.

## Relevance to our research question
Direct dairy HPP review for microbial vs quality trade-offs, including human-milk immunoglobulin examples.

## Open questions / things to verify
