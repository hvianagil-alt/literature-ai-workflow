# From Concept to Commercialization: Unlocking the Potential of High-Pressure Thermal Processing

- **Citation (as given in the paper / filename):** Knoerzer K, Sevenich R. 2025. Food Engineering Reviews 17:627–644. doi:10.1007/s12393-025-09414-9
- **Source file:** papers/2026-09-20-scopus-hpp/extracted/Knoerzer2025627.txt
- **Extracted:** 2026-09-20

## Research question

What scientific principles, equipment, process parameters, quality/contaminant outcomes, and commercialization paths (including canister retrofits of conventional HPP) define high-pressure thermal processing (HPTP / PATS / PATP / PES) as a route to spore-safe, higher-quality shelf-stable foods?

## Methods

Open-access narrative review (Crown 2025). Covers adiabatic compression heating (thermodynamic Eq. 1 and ANN prediction), historical FDA milestones, applications by food class, dedicated vs canister equipment, packaging, process parameters (P, T, time, matrix), FPC reduction, and pressure–temperature synergy (including Olivier et al. “diving bell” spore work). No new primary dataset (“No datasets were generated or analysed”). CSIRO internal appropriation funding.

## Sample / data

Not applicable as primary n. Equipment examples: CSIRO 35 L vessel 690 MPa / 130 °C; AZTI 55 L ~630 MPa / 130 °C; lab vessels 0.1–5 L; canister prototypes up to 525 L Hiperbaric HPP. Compression-heating table (Ting et al.): water/mashed potato/orange juice/tomato salsa/2% fat milk ~3.0 °C/100 MPa at 25 °C; salmon ~3.2; chicken fat ~4.5; beef fat ~6.3; olive oil 8.7 to <6.3; soy oil 9.1 to <6.2.

## Claim-ready facts

- **Design:** Narrative engineering/food-science review of HPTP (PATS/PATP/PES)
- **Population / model:** Low-acid and other foods (juices, dairy, meats/seafood, RTE meals, purées/sauces) plus cited spore and model-food studies
- **n:** not reported (review; authors state no datasets generated)
- **Intervention / comparator:** Combined high pressure (typically 100–600 MPa, often ~600 MPa) plus moderate-to-high temperature (~60–121 °C or higher under pressure) vs conventional retort/pasteurization and vs ambient-temperature HPP
- **Primary endpoint:** Commercial sterility (authors cite 12-log10 spore inactivation target), F0-equivalent lethality, quality retention, and food-processing contaminant (acrylamide, furan, HMF) levels
- **Primary result:** HPTP uses volumetric adiabatic heating (rule of thumb ~3 °C/100 MPa in water-rich foods; fats higher) plus decompression cooling so time above 100 °C can be well under 5–10 min vs >60 min in a retort. Hold times for low-acid sterilization often ~3–5 min at >120 °C. Example: baby-food purée ~3 min at pressure for F0 equivalent of 7 min vs retort requiring product >121 °C for >20 min. FDA accepted a PATS mashed-potato process (2009) and PES for multi-component shelf-stable foods (2015). At writing, no commercial HPTP-labeled retail products; multilayer canisters (inner HDPE, intermediate PP, outer PTFE/insulator) held ~120 °C with <1 °C drop over 5 min; commercial canister availability stated for 2024.
- **Cannot show:** Cannot prove market adoption, a universal 12-log industrial validation, or that all HPP plants can run HPTP without throughput/packaging limits.

## Key findings

- HPTP: typically 100–600 MPa with ~60–121 °C (or higher under pressure) to inactivate spores/enzymes with shorter heat than retorting. (Introduction)
- Compression heating is generally nonlinear; only water at 50 °C initial T was linear in Knoerzer et al. work. ANN trained on 100–600 MPa liquids/solids predicts peak T. (Adiabatic heating; ANN)
- Table 1 (Ting et al. [38]): ~3 °C/100 MPa for water and many high-moisture foods at 25 °C; oils ~6–9 °C/100 MPa and often decrease as P rises. (Table 1)
- Historical: Gould & Sale spore germination by pressure; 2009 FDA PATS mashed potatoes; 2015 PES multi-component foods. (Historical development)
- Dedicated industrial ~100+ L HPTP machines were not operational in industry at writing; few pilots (CSIRO 35 L, AZTI 55 L) have temperature-uniformity issues. (Equipment)
- Canister: inner high-kC polymer (HDPE) heats with compression; PP intermediate; PTFE/low-k outer insulator; outer surface <20 °C while interior ~120 °C. Prototypes for 525 L Hiperbaric; commercial 2024 [9]. (Canister)
- Packaging: ~15% volume compression at 600 MPa; flexible high-T pouches; avoid large air pockets. (Packaging)
- Parameters: 400–600 MPa typical (some 700–800 MPa); initial T often 60–90 °C to reach 90–121 °C peak; hold seconds to ~15 min; pressurization 1–2 min, decompression seconds. C. botulinum processes often ~600 MPa. (Process parameters)
- FPCs: De Vleeschouwer et al. — almost no acrylamide at 600 MPa/110 °C vs ready formation at 110 °C ambient in asparagine–glucose model. Sevenich et al. — furan 50–80% lower than retort (up to 90% in one case); vegetable purée 600 MPa/120 °C little/no furan vs 121 °C ambient. Mixed vegetable baby food: retort 115 °C / 28 min had low HMF vs HPTS 600 MPa brief 121 °C >95% HMF reduction. (Contaminants)
- Synergy: Olivier et al. Bacillus amyloliquefaciens spores at 115–117 °C almost fully inactivated at 600 MPa vs little/no kill with heat alone (diving-bell dual-sample design). Synergy can diminish above ~120 °C. (Synergy)
- Authors: at manuscript time, commercialization of HPTP products was still absent despite regulatory examples. (Applications)

## Limitations (as stated by the authors, or evident from the methods)

- Scale-up: temperature non-uniformity in dedicated pilots; canister volume penalty and extra handling.
- Economics, validation-at-scale, and packaging rated for both 600 MPa and ~120 °C remain barriers.
- Pressure above ~700 MPa increases wear with limited extra microbial benefit once T limits lethality.
- Review synthesizes prior work; ANN and CFD claims depend on cited training/experimental setups.

## Relevance to our research question

Defines HPTP as the thermal hurdle on HPP for spores and shelf-stable low-acid foods, including equipment (canisters) that convert existing HPP lines — central to HPP vs hurdle/HPTP scope.
