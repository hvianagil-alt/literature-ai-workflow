# SU-Eohyeol Pharmacopuncture Ameliorates Parkinson’s Disease–Associated Pain via the CB1 and PPARγ Pathways in an MPTP-Induced Mouse Model

- **Citation:** Kim JI, Yeo H, Jo Y, Kim H, Park S-M, Kim NS; guest editor Chae Y. Pain Res Manag. 2026;2026:3334432. doi: 10.1155/prm/3334432
- **Source file:** papers/2026-09-20-pd-cannabinoids/Kim2026.txt
- **Extracted:** 2026-09-20

## Research question
Does SU-Eohyeol pharmacopuncture (SUEHP) at GB34 reduce MPTP-induced mechanical pain and nigral injury in mice, and do CB1 or PPARγ antagonists block those effects?

## Methods
Male C57BL/6N mice: four i.p. MPTP injections 20 mg/kg every 2 h (Day 0). From 1 week later, SUEHP 20 µL/acupoint, 3 mm depth, twice weekly × 4 at GB34 or a gluteal nonrelevant point; saline-at-GB34 and amitriptyline 10 mg/kg i.p. comparators. Inhibitor study: SR141716A 3 mg/kg (CB1) or T0070907 5 mg/kg (PPARγ) i.p. 30 min before SUEHP. von Frey, open field, rotarod; TH DAB; spinal c-Fos, p-p38, CD11b; BDNF IF; midbrain/spinal RNA-seq. In silico: oleamide → CB1 (1.14 µM PubChem response) and PPARγ; 9-octadecenoic acid → PPARγ. ANOVA + Fisher LSD; p < 0.05.

## Sample / data
8-week-old males. Behavior/body weight n = 10; DAB TH n = 5 (first experiment) or n = 4 (inhibitor IHC); spinal c-Fos/p-p38 n = 5 then n = 4; BDNF n = 4. Exact total animals not summed in text.

## Claim-ready facts
- **Design:** preclinical in vivo (MPTP mouse + pharmacopuncture; antagonist rescue)
- **Population / model:** male C57BL/6N mice; acute MPTP 4 × 20 mg/kg
- **n:** 10/group for von Frey (except staining n = 4–5)
- **Intervention / comparator:** SUEHP at GB34 vs SUEHP at nonrelevant site vs saline vs amitriptyline; ± SR141716A or T0070907
- **Primary endpoint:** mechanical paw-withdrawal threshold (von Frey, g) and spinal c-Fos; secondary TH+ nigral cells
- **Primary result:** MPTP increased mechanical hypersensitivity lasting >21 days; SUEHP at GB34 significantly reduced pain vs MPTP-vehicle (p values marked vs MPTP-Veh: b p < 0.05 to bbb p < 0.001) while SUEHP at the control point and amitriptyline did not (Figures 1d–e). Absolute PWT grams are not stated in the text. CB1 or PPARγ blockade eliminated the analgesic and TH-recovery effects (Figures 4–5).
- **Cannot show:** that SUEHP is a purified cannabinoid drug; human PD pain trials; that motor recovery is robust (open-field distance not significant; discussion also states motor deficits were not significantly improved).

## Key findings
- GB34 SUEHP, not gluteal SUEHP or amitriptyline, mitigated MPTP loss of nigral TH+ cells and striatal TH intensity (Figure 1f–h; n = 5).
- Spinal dorsal-horn c-Fos and p-p38 rose after MPTP and fell with GB34 SUEHP; microglia (CD11b) decreased; astrocytes (GFAP) unchanged (Figure 2; n = 5).
- Rotarod latency reduced by MPTP was ameliorated by SUEHP; SR141716A but not T0070907 eliminated that motor benefit (Supporting Figure; text Section 3.5).
- Midbrain RNA-seq: 2525 DEGs vs MPTP-vehicle (935 up, 1590 down); spinal cord 111 DEGs; antagonist co-treatment typically reversed inflammation-related GSEA (Figure 7).
- SN BDNF restored by SUEHP; SR141716A or T0070907 blocked SN BDNF recovery (n = 4); hippocampal BDNF recovery was not blocked by those antagonists (Section 3.6).

## Limitations
- Male mice only; acute MPTP, not progressive synucleinopathy (authors’ own limitations).
- SUEHP is a multi-herb plus deer-antler injectate; CB1/PPARγ assignment is from oleamide/9-octadecenoic acid in silico, not CBD/THC.
- Amitriptyline failed as a pain positive control in this model.
- Figure legends contain “GB35” typos for some antagonist groups.
- Discussion vs results conflict on whether motor function improved.

## Relevance to our research question
Not a phytocannabinoid monotherapy, but a PD toxin (MPTP) pain study whose analgesia and TH preservation require CB1 and PPARγ. Relevant as endocannabinoid/PPAR pharmacology in a PD model, not as clinical cannabis.

## Open questions / things to verify

- Reconcile Section 3.5 rotarod improvement with Discussion claim of non-significant motor benefit.
- Whether oleamide in the injectate is sufficient to engage brain CB1 after 20 µL acupoint dosing.
