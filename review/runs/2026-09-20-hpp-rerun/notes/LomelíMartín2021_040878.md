# Induced Changes in Aroma Compounds of Foods Treated with High Hydrostatic Pressure: A Review

- **Citation (as given in the paper / filename):** Lomelí-Martín A, Martínez LM, Welti-Chanes J, Escobedo-Avellaneda Z. Foods. 2021;10(4):878. doi:10.3390/foods10040878. PMC8072623.
- **Source file:** papers/2026-09-20-hpp-rerun/LomelíMartín2021_040878.txt
- **Extracted:** 2026-09-20

## Research question
How does high hydrostatic pressure (HHP, 100–1000 MPa) change lipid-derived aroma compounds (aldehydes, alcohols, ketones, esters, lactones, terpenes, phenols) in plant and animal foods relative to untreated or thermally processed products?

## Methods
Narrative review of HHP effects on aroma classes. Compares HHP with thermal processing when the source papers did. No original chromatograms.

## Sample / data
Cited matrices include red plum puree, kiwifruit pulp, mulberry juice, Keitt mango juice, green asparagus juice, germinated brown rice, and other vegetable/animal foods. Pressure window in tables typically 100–800 MPa.

## Claim-ready facts
- **Design:** narrative review of HHP aroma chemistry
- **Population / model:** vegetable and animal foods in the cited volatile studies
- **n:** not applicable
- **Intervention / comparator:** HHP ~100–1000 MPa (examples 200–600 MPa, 1 s to 20 min) vs untreated and, where reported, thermal processing
- **Primary endpoint:** relative change in class totals or named volatiles (area or concentration vs unprocessed)
- **Primary result (compiled):** Across most cited matrices, total aldehydes increased 3–261% and alcohols decreased 4.1–90.9% after 100–600 MPa. Red plum puree: total aldehyde AAU ↑3.8–7.2% except 400 MPa/1 s (↓0.8%); max ↑7.2% at 400 MPa/5 min; hexanal >75% of aldehyde area and unaffected. Kiwifruit pulp: most aldehydes ↑5–102.2%; largest at 500 MPa/10 min (↑8.7–102.2%); alcohols ↓5.3% at 400 MPa and ↓24.7% at 600 MPa. Green asparagus juice: aldehydes ↑25.3–66.1% (max 200 MPa/20 min). Germinated brown rice 100–500 MPa/15 min: aldehydes ↑13.6–100.2%. Keitt mango juice: C9 aldehydes ((E)-2-nonenal, (E,Z)-2,6-nonadienal) fell above 400 MPa. One set of treatments decreased (E)-2-heptenal, benzaldehyde, and (E)-2-nonenal by 42.6–82.1%. Ketones generally increased or were unchanged (kiwifruit an exception). Esters decreased in most matrices. Lactones mostly stable below 400 MPa and often declined at ≥500 MPa. Terpene concentration had no clear tendency. Phenols decreased, attributed to oxidation. HHP is classified as nonthermal because adiabatic heating with water as medium does not exceed 50 °C (Introduction).
- **Cannot show:** a single aroma “fingerprint” for all HHP foods; microbial safety; enzyme activity measured in this paper; that 400 MPa/5 min equals 600 MPa/15 min for flavour.

## Key findings
- Mechanism (proposed from literature): HHP does not break covalent bonds below 1000–2000 MPa, so primary structures of aroma molecules stay intact; secondary–quaternary enzyme structures and volume-decreasing reactions change, so residual enzymes and oxidation during storage can raise aldehydes (HHP section).
- Author interpretation: aldehyde/ketone increases vs alcohol/phenol/ester/lactone decreases are typical but matrix- and cycle-specific; optimisation remains an open task (Abstract).

## Limitations (as stated by the authors, or evident from the methods)
- Heterogeneous GC methods (AAU vs concentration); ranges such as 3–261% mix many foods.
- Residual enzyme activity after HHP is inferred, not assayed here.
- Storage after HHP, not only the hold, is credited for some oxidation.

## Relevance to our research question
Intake related paper from OpenAlex related-to-seeds search (not a post-table gap-fill). Quality endpoint: a named HHP cycle does **not** freeze the aroma profile. Aldehydes often rise and esters/alcohols fall, and mango C9 aldehydes move opposite to asparagus or plum puree at similar 400–600 MPa holds.

## Open questions / things to verify
The 3–261% aldehyde range is a review synthesis; use matrix-specific percentages (Table 2) when citing a food. Terpene “no clear tendency” is the authors’ summary, not a null statistical test.
