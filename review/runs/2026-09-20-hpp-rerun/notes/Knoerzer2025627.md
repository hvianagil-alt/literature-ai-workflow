# From Concept to Commercialization: Unlocking the Potential of High-Pressure Thermal Processing

- **Citation (as given in the paper / filename):** Knoerzer K, Sevenich R. Food Engineering Reviews. 2025;17:627–644. doi:10.1007/s12393-025-09414-9
- **Source file:** papers/2026-09-20-hpp-rerun/Knoerzer2025627.txt
- **Extracted:** 2026-09-20

## Research question
How does high-pressure thermal processing (HPTP) use adiabatic compression heating plus moderate-to-high temperature to inactivate spores and enzymes while limiting quality loss, and what equipment/process windows make that commercially plausible?

## Methods
Narrative engineering review of HPTP (also PATS/PATP/PES). No new inactivation dataset. Synthesizes thermodynamic compression-heating relations, CFD/ANN modelling, pilot equipment, multilayer canister technology, and cited spore/quality/contaminant studies.

## Sample / data
Not a primary experiment. Cited windows: pressure typically 100–600 MPa (operations often 400–600 MPa; some studies 700–800 MPa) with initial temperatures about 60 °C up to 121 °C or higher under pressure. Pilot vessels: CSIRO 35 L (690 MPa, 130 °C); AZTI 55 L (~630 MPa, 130 °C). FDA acceptance of a PATS mashed-potato process in 2009; PES for multi-component shelf-stable foods in 2015. Authors state no commercial HPTP food products were on the market at writing; canister prototypes for up to 525 L Hiperbaric HPP systems tested, commercial availability 2024.

## Claim-ready facts

- **Design:** narrative review of HPTP science, equipment and scale-up (no new trial)
- **Population / model:** low-acid shelf-stable foods (juices pH >4.6, dairy, meats/seafood, RTE meals, fruit/vegetable purées) where bacterial spores must be inactivated
- **n:** not applicable; compression-heating table values and cited D-value/hold-time examples come from prior studies
- **Intervention / comparator:** HPTP (high hydrostatic pressure + elevated temperature, adiabatic heating) vs conventional retort/pasteurization; optionally using insulated multilayer canisters inside ambient HPP machines
- **Primary endpoint:** spore lethality (including 12-log10 commercial sterility / C. botulinum), hold time above ~100–121 °C, adiabatic ΔT, formation of acrylamide/furan/HMF, quality retention vs retort
- **Primary result:** HPTP mechanism is adiabatic compression heating: work of compression raises product temperature, ideally uniformly. Rule of thumb for water-rich foods ~3 °C per 100 MPa from room temperature (~18 °C at 600 MPa); fats heat more (chicken fat ~4.5, beef fat ~6.3, olive oil 8.7 to <6.3, soy oil 9.1 to <6.2 °C/100 MPa; Table 1, substances at 25 °C). Linearity is the exception (water linear only from a 50 °C start in Knoerzer et al.). Typical process: preheat 60–90 °C so that 400–600 MPa reaches ~90–121 °C; hold often 3–5 min at >120 °C for 12-log10 C. botulinum, vs retort 15–30 min at 121 °C in the cold spot; time above 100 °C can be <5–10 min vs >60 min in retort. Pressure 50–600 MPa can trigger spore germination; then heat under pressure kills germinated spores. Olivier et al.: B. amyloliquefaciens spores at 115–117 °C were almost completely inactivated at 600 MPa but little/no kill with heat alone. Example: baby-food purée ~3 min at pressure for F0 equivalent of 7 min vs >20 min >121 °C in retort. Acrylamide: almost none detected at 600 MPa/110 °C vs ready formation at 110 °C ambient (De Vleeschouwer et al., 400–700 MPa, 100–115 °C). Furan 50–80% lower than retort; up to 90% reduction in one cited case; HMF >95% reduction in a mixed vegetable baby food (115 °C/28 min retort vs 600 MPa briefly at 121 °C). Three-layer canister (HDPE inner / PP / PTFE outer) held ~120 °C with <1 °C drop over a 5 min hold while the outer surface stayed <20 °C. Package volume compresses ~15% at 600 MPa.
- **Cannot show:** that ambient (non-thermal) HPP inactivates spores — this review is about adding heat; that commercial HPTP foods already exist (authors say they do not); a single universal MPa/°C/min that equals 12-log sterility for every food (product-specific heating and resistance).

## Key findings
- Measurement: water, mashed potato, orange juice, tomato salsa and 2% fat milk all ~3.0 °C/100 MPa at 25 °C start; oils several-fold higher (Table 1).
- Measurement: synergy example — same thermal path, 600 MPa vs ambient pressure, markedly more B. amyloliquefaciens spore kill (Olivier et al.; Figure 9).
- Author interpretation: HPTP is a high-temperature short-time sterilization for solids/particulates in-package, with less acrylamide/furan than retort, but still no industrial dedicated ~100+ L HPTP machine at writing.
- Mechanism grade: adiabatic heating is **thermodynamically defined and experimentally measured in cited work**; spore germination by pressure then heat kill is **cited** (Gould & Sale 1970; Reineke et al.); contaminant reductions are **cited experiments**, not new data.

## Limitations (as stated by the authors, or evident from the methods)
- Review, no new spore kinetics generated.
- Dedicated HPTP pilots have temperature-uniformity problems; canister slightly cuts vessel fill.
- Above ~120 °C, extra benefit of pressure can diminish for some spores; very high pressure can even stabilize some proteins.
- No commercial products yet despite 2009/2015 FDA acceptances.

## Relevance to our research question
Defines the boundary where heat *is* part of the kill step: ambient HPP does not do what HPTP does to spores. A named 600 MPa cycle at chill/ambient temperature is not the same as 600 MPa after a 60–90 °C preheat that peaks near 121 °C for 3–5 min.

## Open questions / things to verify
- Compression-heating coefficients are material- and T/P-dependent; the 3 °C/100 MPa rule is a water-rich approximation, not a food-universal constant (authors stress non-linearity).
- F0 and 12-log10 figures are from cited PATS/baby-food studies, not a new validation in this paper.
