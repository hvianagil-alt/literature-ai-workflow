# High Hydrostatic Pressure to Increase the Biosynthesis and Extraction of Phenolic Compounds in Food: A Review

- **Citation (as given in the paper / filename):** Navarro-Baez JE, Martínez LM, Welti-Chanes J, Buitimea-Cantúa GV, Escobedo-Avellaneda Z. Molecules. 2022;27(5):1502. doi:10.3390/molecules27051502. PMC8911777.
- **Source file:** papers/2026-09-20-hpp-rerun/NavarroBaez2022_051502.txt
- **Extracted:** 2026-09-20

## Research question
When HHP raises phenolic content in fruits, vegetables, or cultures, is that biosynthesis (low-pressure stress, PAL/ROS) or better extraction after cell disruption (higher pressure), and what pressure–time windows separate those mechanisms?

## Methods
Narrative review. Separates (i) HHP as abiotic elicitor (typically 15–100 MPa, 10–20 min, room temperature, increments during storage) from (ii) extractability at 300–600 MPa / 5–20 min after cells are no longer viable. Discusses shikimate pathway and PAL. No original HHP run.

## Sample / data
Cited systems: whole mango, carrot, strawberry; *Vitis vinifera* and potato suspension cultures; apricot nectar, sour cherry pomace, prickly pear (microscopy), and other plant tissues in Table 2.

## Claim-ready facts
- **Design:** narrative review of HHP effects on phenolic biosynthesis vs extraction
- **Population / model:** fruits, vegetables, and plant cell cultures in the cited studies
- **n:** not applicable
- **Intervention / comparator:** low HHP (~10–200 MPa) vs high HHP (typically 300–600 MPa, sometimes 500–600 MPa for wall collapse) vs untreated; storage at specified T/RH
- **Primary endpoint:** % change in total phenols, flavonoids, anthocyanins vs untreated; PAL activity where reported
- **Primary result (compiled):** Treatments 15–100 MPa for 10–20 min at room temperature produced phenolic biosynthesis changes with increments up to 155% (Abstract). Whole mango 15–60 MPa/10–20 min, then 2–14 days at 25 °C, 85–90% RH: total phenols ↓7.2 to ↑68.4%; flavonoids ↓38.6 to ↑36.8% (PAL not reported). Mango 20–80 MPa/10 min, 13 °C storage: phenols ↓5.2 to ↑30%; flavonoids ↓27.6 to ↑69.7%. Mango 60 MPa/20 min: phenols ↑ about 11, 29, and 47% after 2, 5, and 8 days vs untreated, then decline. Hu et al. mango: +19.6% phenols after 1 day; +69.7% flavonoids after 4 days. Carrots 60 and 100 MPa (come-up 15.33 and 20.67 s) at 22 °C: total phenols ↓11.8 to ↑154.9%; PAL ↓61.4 to ↑380%. After 1 day, phenols +69.1% (60 MPa) and +154.9% (100 MPa), then fell toward control. PAL on day 0 reduced 61.4% at 100 MPa; on day 2 PAL +380.2% (60 MPa) and +139.7% (100 MPa). *V. vinifera* culture 40 MPa/10 min: anthocyanin ↓53.9 to ↑53.3% (max day 6). Potato culture 100–200 MPa/10 min: polyphenols immediately +54, +81, +267, +456, and +453% at 100, 125, 150, 175, and 200 MPa — attributed to vacuole release, **not** biosynthesis. Strawberry 30–90 MPa/5 min: total phenols ↑6.4 to ↑23.1%; anthocyanin ↓16.9 to ↑10.0%. Apricot nectar 300–500 MPa/5–20 min: TPC ↑2.0 to ↑12.5%. Prickly pear microscopy: 100 MPa still viable with wall-bound phenol release; 350–600 MPa collapsed walls and extraction rose with P and time. Authors state 500–600 MPa causes cell-wall disruption that releases phenolics (Abstract).
- **Cannot show:** that low-pressure HHP is a microbial kill step; a universal 155% gain; PAL-proven biosynthesis in the mango papers they criticise for missing PAL/ROS/gene data.

## Key findings
- Mechanism (graded): Immediate response = wall deformation, respiration, H2O2 signalling (proposed from cited physiology). Late response = PAL-linked phenolic biosynthesis during storage (measured PAL only in the carrot study of Viacava et al.). Above ~100 MPa, cells are described as inactivated before they can mount biosynthesis, so extra phenolics are extraction (Section 3.2).
- Author interpretation: increment is not proportional to pressure; fruit type, ripening, and storage T/RH dominate. Oxidative enzymes that survive <200 MPa can later consume the extra phenolics.

## Limitations (as stated by the authors, or evident from the methods)
- Several “biosynthesis” papers lack PAL, ROS, or gene-expression assays.
- Potato culture increases at 100–200 MPa are extraction artefacts.
- HHP at preservation pressures does not reliably inactivate all enzymes or spores (Introduction).
- Bound vs free phenolic operational definitions vary by solvent.

## Relevance to our research question
Intake related paper from OpenAlex related-to-seeds search (not a post-table gap-fill). Directly answers “a named cycle is not the same quality outcome”: 60–100 MPa can elicit up to ~155% more carrot phenols during storage, whereas 500–600 MPa is an extraction/disruption regime, and 100–200 MPa on potato cultures dumps vacuolar phenolics without biosynthesis.
