# The Effect of Non-Thermal Processing on the Fate of Pathogenic Bacteria and Hidden Hazardous Risks

- **Citation (as given in the paper / filename):** Wu Y, Li X, Ma X, Ren Q, Sun Z, Pan H. Foods. 2025;14(13):2374. doi:10.3390/foods14132374
- **Source file:** papers/2026-09-20-hpp-rerun/Wu2025.txt
- **Extracted:** 2026-09-20

## Research question
How do non-thermal processes (HPP, PEF, CAP, HPCD, ultrasound) inactivate foodborne bacteria, and when do they instead leave cells in a viable but nonculturable (VBNC) state that plate counts miss?

## Methods
Narrative review of published inactivation and VBNC studies across five non-thermal technologies. HPP is covered first (Section 2), with a tabulated compilation of log reductions by food and organism (Table 2). Mechanisms are proposed from cited experimental papers (membrane, ribosome, protein unfolding); the review itself does not generate new inactivation data.

## Sample / data
No primary experimental n. Table 2 compiles HPP inactivation from multiple food matrices (juices, shellfish, meat, dairy, puree). HPP industrial pressure window stated as 100–800 MPa (Section 2.1).

## Claim-ready facts

- **Design:** narrative review of non-thermal microbial inactivation and VBNC risk (no new experiment)
- **Population / model:** foodborne bacteria and viruses in human foods (juices, meat, dairy, produce, shellfish); HPP is one of five technologies reviewed
- **n:** not applicable (review); tabulated HPP examples are cited studies, not a pooled n
- **Intervention / comparator:** HPP typically 100–800 MPa vs thermal pasteurization/sterilization and vs PEF, CAP, HPCD, ultrasound; some cited HPP studies combine pressure with freezing, nisin, CO2, or mild heat
- **Primary endpoint:** log inactivation (log N0/N) of target organisms; discrepancy between plate counts and viability assays as evidence of VBNC
- **Primary result:** HPP log reductions in Table 2 range from 0.1–1.5 (norovirus in kimchi, 100–400 MPa) to >5–9 log depending on organism, matrix, and cycle. Named examples: human norovirus >3 log at 600 MPa/1 °C/2 min (green onions and salsa) and >4 log at 450 MPa/1 °C/5 min (oysters and clams); L. innocua and S. cerevisiae >5 log in pitaya juice at 550 MPa/16 min or 600 MPa/12 min; V. parahaemolyticus 9 log in TSB+2.5% NaCl at 300 MPa/10 min and 5 log in oysters at ≥350 MPa/2 min; Salmonella enterica and L. monocytogenes >6.7 log in cantaloupe puree at 500 MPa/8 °C/5 min; Salmonella Typhimurium 4 log in raw chicken breast at 400 MPa/30 °C/1 min; E. coli O157:H7 6.4–8.34 log in carrot and grapefruit juices at 615 MPa/15 °C/2 min. Sublethal HPP can leave VBNC cells: Ritz et al. reported 8-log loss of culturability of L. monocytogenes and S. Typhimurium at 400–600 MPa/2 °C/10 min but ~4-log surviving cells by direct viable count, which resuscitated at 4 °C and 20 °C; Karamova et al. reported >7-log drop in culturable S. Typhimurium at 300 MPa/23 °C/15 min versus only 0.72-log reduction by PI-excluding flow cytometry. Berlin et al. (200–300 MPa, 25 °C, 5–15 min) inactivated pathogenic Vibrio without inducing VBNC. Yang et al. reported a positive correlation between pressure resistance and resuscitable VBNC populations; combining HPP with CO2 or nisin reduced RVBNC formation.
- **Cannot show:** a single HPP pressure–time that is equivalent across foods or organisms; spore kill by ambient-temperature HPP (not the review’s focus); that plate-count “complete inactivation” equals absence of viable cells; pooled effect sizes or a ranked safety outcome for a named cycle.

## Key findings
- Measurement: HPP is described as acting on non-covalent bonds (hydrogen, ionic, hydrophobic), disrupting membranes, dissociating ribosomes, and aggregating higher-order protein structure, while covalent primary structure is left intact (Section 2.2).
- Measurement: Table 2 shows the same named pressure is not a fixed log kill: 400 MPa gave 4 log Salmonella in chicken in 1 min, <1–5 log in other cited meat/juice combinations, and only 0.1–1.5 log norovirus in kimchi at 100–400 MPa.
- Author interpretation: VBNC is induced under sublethal rather than fully lethal HPP; lethal exposures inactivate rather than convert cells to VBNC (Section 2.3).
- Author interpretation: HPP and PEF are commercially applied with relatively small physicochemical change; CAP/HPCD/US-induced VBNC cells may remain virulent (Table 7; Section 8).
- Mechanism grade: HPP membrane/ribosome/protein effects are **cited from earlier experimental work**, not newly measured here. VBNC induction under HPP is **cited** (Ritz, Karamova, Yang, Shangguan), not generated by this paper.

## Limitations (as stated by the authors, or evident from the methods)
- Narrative compilation, not a systematic review or meta-analysis.
- Table 1 has garbled units in the extracted text (e.g. plate-count rows labelled mg/mL); those cells were not used.
- VBNC research is described as inconsistent across technologies (especially PEF) and mostly theoretical (Section 9).
- HPP equipment cost is listed as a practical limit (Table 7).

## Relevance to our research question
Directly addresses when a named HPP cycle is not the same safety outcome: culture-based “kill” can hide VBNC survivors that resuscitate, and log reductions at 300–600 MPa differ by organism, matrix, and whether pressure is combined with heat, freezing, or antimicrobials.
