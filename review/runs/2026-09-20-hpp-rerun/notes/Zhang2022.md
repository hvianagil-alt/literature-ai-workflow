# Recent Progress in the Synergistic Bactericidal Effect of High Pressure and Temperature Processing in Fruits and Vegetables and Related Kinetics

- **Citation (as given in the paper / filename):** Zhang S, Meenu M, Hu L, Ren J, Ramaswamy HS, Yu Y. Foods. 2022;11(22):3698. doi:10.3390/foods11223698. PMID:36429290. PMC9689688.
- **Source file:** papers/2026-09-20-hpp-rerun/Zhang2022.txt
- **Extracted:** 2026-09-20
- **Screening:** Included (narrative review of HPP / high-pressure thermal processing [HPTP] inactivation kinetics in fruit and vegetable systems)

## Research question
How do high-pressure processing (HPP) and high-pressure thermal processing (HPTP) inactivate vegetative cells and spores in fruit and vegetable products, which kinetic models fit those curves, and which factors (species, growth stage, pressure mode, temperature including sub-zero, soluble solids, additives, water activity, pH) change the outcome of a named pressure–time cycle?

## Methods
Narrative review of primary kinetic and inactivation studies in juices, purees, and related fruit/vegetable matrices. Compiles first-order, Weibull, secondary Z_T/Z_P, and polynomial/RSM models; tabulated log reductions and D-values from cited experiments (Tables 1–3). No new experiment.

## Sample / data
Not a primary dataset. Tables compile published cycles across *E. coli*, *L. monocytogenes*, *Bacillus coagulans* spores, *Alicyclobacillus acidoterrestris* spores, *Neosartorya fischeri* ascospores, *Byssochlamys nivea* ascospores, and yeasts in mango, açaí, tomato, orange, peach, apple, strawberry, bayberry, and carrot juices/purees.

## Claim-ready facts
- **Design:** narrative review of HPP and HPTP bactericidal kinetics in fruit and vegetable foods (not a new trial)
- **Population / model:** microorganisms in fruit and vegetable juices, purees, and broths as compiled from cited studies
- **n:** not reported as a pooled sample size; review of published kinetic datasets (Tables 1–3)
- **Intervention / comparator:** HPP (typically 200–600 MPa, some laboratory cycles to 1000–1200 MPa) versus HPTP (pressure plus moderate heat, often 40–95 °C) versus thermal-only or unfrozen versus frozen/sub-zero pressure; single-pulse versus multi-pulse HPP
- **Primary endpoint:** log CFU reduction, decimal reduction time D_{T,P} (min), and Weibull parameters b and n
- **Primary result:** Vegetative spoilage/pathogens are generally inactivated at 400–600 MPa, but pasteurization is stated to require at least 5–6 log cfu/g or mL; spores often survive conventional industrial pressures (<600 MPa) at ambient temperature, and *B. subtilis* spores survived up to 1200 MPa at ambient temperature (Introduction). Same named cycle is not equivalent across matrices: *L. monocytogenes* 4a KUEN 136 first-order D-values at 25 °C were 2.87 min (300 MPa), 1.80 min (400 MPa), and 0.87 min (600 MPa) in orange juice versus 6.17, 3.39, and 1.52 min in peach juice (Table 1). *E. coli* D-values in frozen carrot juice at −20 °C were 4.03 min (200 MPa) down to 2.12 min (400 MPa), versus 28.53 min (300 MPa), 9.20 min (350 MPa), and 5.32 min (400 MPa) in unfrozen carrot juice at 4 °C (Table 1). Zhu et al. as cited: 1.87 log cfu/mL reduction of *E. coli* in unfrozen carrot juice versus 6.80 log cfu/mL in frozen carrot juice after 330 MPa for 10 min (Section 3.3.2). Frozen orange juice (−80 °C) at 250 MPa/15 min: 4.88 log cfu/mL *E. coli* reduction versus 0.42 log without freezing (Table 3). *A. acidoterrestris* spores in orange juice: <1 log at 600 MPa/45 °C/10 min, 1.2 log at 55 °C, 2.7 log at 65 °C; 200 MPa/65 °C gave 1.7 log and 0.1 MPa/65 °C gave no listed reduction (Table 3). *N. fischeri* ascospores in apple juice: no reduction at 0.1 MPa/75 °C versus 4.8 log at 600 MPa/75 °C (Table 3); text also cites 3.3 log cfu/mL at 600 MPa/75 °C/10 min versus no thermal-only reduction (Section 3.3.1). *B. coagulans* ATCC 7050 spores in tomato pulp: 2.5 log at 300 MPa/60 °C/20 min versus 6.5 log at 600 MPa/60 °C/20 min (Table 3). Soluble solids protected *A. acidoterrestris* spores: D-values 4.17, 7.59, and 13.71 min at 10, 20, and 30 °Brix (Section 3.4). Pokhrel et al. as cited: >5 log of both *L. innocua* and *E. coli* in carrot juice at 300 MPa/35 °C/2 min with natural bacteriocins versus <1 log without (Section 3.4).
- **Cannot show:** a single validated industrial cycle for all fruit/vegetable products; this paper does not generate new inactivation data, does not pool studies statistically, and does not measure quality/enzyme endpoints as a primary experiment. Kinetics compiled from other foods cannot be used without matrix-specific validation (Conclusions).

## Key findings
- First-order kinetics fit some linear HPP/HPTP survivor curves; Weibull is preferred when shoulder/tailing occurs because of resistant subpopulations or sublethal injury (Section 2.1). Hossein et al.: Weibull best for *B. coagulans* 185A spores in tomato juice under HPTP, while first-order fit thermal-only processing (Section 2.1; Table 1: 600 MPa/75 °C, b = 0.87, n = 0.79, R² = 0.950).
- Pressure-tolerance order is generally spores > Gram-positive (*L. monocytogenes*, *S. aureus*) > fungi > Gram-negative (*E. coli*), but the order can reverse with matrix protection; stationary-phase cells are more pressure-tolerant than logarithmic-phase cells (Section 3.1).
- In the same mango juice at 300 MPa/20 °C/5 min, *Zygosaccharomyces bailii* fell 2.7–2.9 log, *Pichia membranaefaciens* 3.7–4.3 log, and *Leuconostoc mesenteroides* <0.5 log (Table 2). In açaí juices at 400 MPa/5 °C/1 min, *L. monocytogenes* fell 6–7 log versus 3–4 log for *E. coli* O157:H7 and *Salmonella* spp. (Table 2).
- Multi-pulse/multi-stage HPP can outperform single static holds for enzymes, yeasts, cells, and some spores; pulse number, pressure, and temperature interact (Section 3.2). Pressurization/depressurization rate also changes lethality (Ratphitagsanti et al. cited).
- Moderate heat (40–90 °C) with pressure increases protein denaturation and lethality; low-temperature/frozen HPP lethality is attributed in cited work to Ice I–Ice III phase-transition mechanical damage plus greater protein and membrane sensitivity below 0 °C (Sections 3.3.1–3.3.2). Mechanism of Ice I/III damage is **cited/proposed**, not newly measured here.
- Low water activity and high soluble solids baroprotect; high acidity increases lethality. Carambola puree pH fell from 4.57 (control) to 4.41 after 800 MPa at 25 °C (Section 3.5).
- Most existing kinetic models omit pH, a_w, and composition, so predictions transfer poorly across products (Section 3.5; Conclusions).

## Limitations (as stated by the authors, or evident from the methods)
- Few studies on low-temperature plus high-pressure kinetics (Conclusions).
- Many papers fit one or two models without optimization, validation, or application; industrial equipment is mostly rated ≤600 MPa and long operation >500 MPa raises maintenance cost (Conclusions).
- Tables inherit numbers from heterogeneous primary studies (strain, come-up time, and holding-time reporting are not standardized in this review).

## Relevance to our research question
Directly addresses when a named MPa–min cycle is not the same safety outcome: frozen versus unfrozen juice, orange versus peach juice D-values, thermal-only versus HPTP for mould ascospores, and bacteriocin hurdles at 300 MPa. Heat is often *not* the sole kill step, but moderate or sub-zero temperature changes HPP lethality.

## Open questions / things to verify
- Table 3 holding times for *N. fischeri* (4.8 log at 600 MPa/75 °C) versus the narrative 3.3 log at 10 min should not be pooled as one cycle without checking the cited primary papers.
- Carambola pH change is quality/chemistry, not a microbial endpoint.
