# High Hydrostatic Pressure: Influences on Allergenicity, Bioactivities, and Structural and Functional Properties of Proteins from Diverse Food Sources

- **Citation (as given in the paper / filename):** Braspaiboon S, Laokuldilok T. Foods. 2024;13(6):922. doi:10.3390/foods13060922. PMID:38540912. PMC10969792.
- **Source file:** papers/2026-09-20-hpp-rerun/Braspaiboon2024.txt
- **Extracted:** 2026-09-20
- **Screening:** Included (narrative review of HHP effects on food proteins: structure, allergenicity, bioactivity, function)

## Research question
How does high hydrostatic pressure (HHP / HPP) change non-covalent protein structure and, through that unfolding, allergenicity, bioactivities, digestibility, and functional properties (hydrophobicity, emulsifying/foaming, gel texture) across cereals, legumes/nuts, seeds, vegetables, dairy/eggs, meat/poultry, seafood, algae, and insects?

## Methods
Narrative review of articles from 2014–2024 (plus some earlier cited primary work) compiling pressure (MPa), time (min), and directional effects (+/−) on structure, IgE/IgG binding, antioxidant/ACE activities, and technofunctional indices (Tables 1–3). No new experiment.

## Sample / data
Not a primary n. Tables catalogue protein extracts and foods (buckwheat, rice, quinoa, wheat gluten, soy, peanut, almond, walnut, sweet potato, whey, casein, egg, beef/chicken products, squid/oyster tropomyosin, surimi, *A. platensis*, mealworm/cricket).

## Claim-ready facts
- **Design:** narrative review of HHP modification of dietary proteins (not a new trial)
- **Population / model:** proteins from cereals, legumes, nuts, seeds, vegetables, milk, eggs, meat, poultry, seafood, algae, and insects as reported in the compiled primary studies
- **n:** not reported as a pooled sample; review of published HHP protein studies (Tables 1–3)
- **Intervention / comparator:** HHP typically 50–900 MPa for ~1–30 min (some extraction holds of hours) versus untreated or thermally treated proteins; some studies combine HHP with hydrolysis
- **Primary endpoint:** directional change in allergenicity (IgE/IgG binding), bioactivity (antioxidant, ACE-inhibitory), surface hydrophobicity, emulsifying/foaming indices, texture (hardness, G′), and digestibility
- **Primary result:** Authors conclude HHP disrupts non-covalent bonds, unfolds interior hydrophobic regions, and commonly increases surface hydrophobicity and antioxidant activity while lowering allergenicity in many plant and seafood proteins; the same MPa is not optimal for every source (Abstract; Section 4). Pasteurization context cited: 600 MPa produced 5–8 log reductions of *E. coli*, *Salmonella enteritidis*, *Listeria monocytogenes*, and *Vibrio* sp.; 400–600 MPa for 5 min used to suppress enzymatic browning in juices (Section 2). Cereals: buckwheat allergenicity reduced 72.2% at 400 MPa for 20 min (Section 3.1.1; Table 1). Almond protein extracts at 400–580 MPa for 3 min showed *increased* immunoreactivity and *decreased* solubility (Table 1). Soy protein isolates at 200–500 MPa for 5–20 min: decreased allergenicity with α-helix/β-sheet loss and increased SH (Table 1). Ginkgo seed proteins: allergenicity decreased with pressure; samples treated above 600 MPa for 20 min showed absence of IgE-binding activities (Section 3.1.3). Dairy: β-lactoglobulin reduced in whey and whole milk at 600 MPa for at least 5 min, attributed to fewer disulfide bonds than α-lactalbumin (Section 3.2.1). Egg: liquid whole egg/white/yolk at 900 MPa for 15 min increased hardness via pressure-induced gelation (Section 3.2.1). Squid tropomyosin Tod p1: IgE/IgG binding notably low at 400 MPa/20 min, no significant further difference at 600 MPa (Section 3.2.3). Oyster tropomyosin: lowest IgG binding at 600 MPa for 5 min (Section 3.2.3). Insect proteins: 380 MPa for 1 min improved Alcalase degree of hydrolysis and reduced allergenicity in one mealworm study, but Dion-Poulin et al. reported *decreased* DH for mealworm (not cricket) under the same 380 MPa/1 min cycle (Section 3.3.2).
- **Cannot show:** a universal pressure–time cycle for simultaneous microbial kill, enzyme inactivation, and protein quality; this review does not generate new microbial D-values or pooled effect sizes. Allergenicity reductions are immunoassay/epitope changes, not clinical desensitization.

## Key findings
- Mechanism **proposed/cited**: non-covalent disruption (H-bonds, hydrophobic and electrostatic interactions) alters α-helix/β-sheet, exposes buried hydrophobics, and can mask epitopes by aggregation (Section 3; Figure 2). Disulfide (covalent) bonds are generally not the primary HHP target.
- Surface hydrophobicity of millet and quinoa proteins decreased at 500–600 MPa/10–15 min, whereas rice and wheat proteins increased at >300 MPa—linked to folding versus unfolding/fluorescence (Section 3.1.1).
- Soy/pea/walnut emulsifying and foaming often improve with unfolding at moderate pressure (often 200–400 MPa); solubility can fall above ~400 MPa from new disulfide-linked aggregates (Section 3.1.2).
- Meat/poultry: HHP increases lightness and reduces redness (myoglobin denaturation/heme release **cited**); hardness/springiness often rise, but cooking/drip loss and TBARS lipid oxidation can worsen (Section 3.2.2).
- Seafood: texture (hardness, springiness, chewiness) often improves above ~100 MPa via hydrogen-bonded gels, unlike heat-set gels (Section 3.2.3).
- Algae: 100–600 MPa up to 20 min can raise soluble protein and C-phycocyanin from *A. platensis*; 400 MPa/20 min increased protein, TPC, and antioxidant activity of red-macroalgae extracts (Section 3.3.1). One *A. platensis* study at 600 MPa/5 min reported *decreased* antioxidant activity despite higher phycobiliproteins (Table 3).
- Authors stress that maximum pressure is not always the best functional outcome (Section 4).

## Limitations (as stated by the authors, or evident from the methods)
- Insect-protein HHP literature is sparse and contradictory on degree of hydrolysis (Section 3.3.2).
- Direction of change (solubility, EAI/ESI, allergenicity) is source- and pressure-specific; tables report signs, not always exact % or p-values.
- Microbial 5–8 log claim at 600 MPa is cited from Silva 2023, not newly measured.

## Relevance to our research question
Shows that a named HHP cycle (e.g. 400 MPa/20 min versus 600 MPa/5 min versus 900 MPa/15 min) is not a single quality or allergenicity outcome: almond immunoreactivity can rise while buckwheat/soy/ginkgo/seafood tropomyosin binding falls; meat WHC can fall while seafood WHC rises.

## Open questions / things to verify
- Many table cells are directional only; exact log reductions, ELISA percentages, and p-values live in the cited primary papers.
- Almond increased immunoreactivity at 400–580 MPa/3 min is a counterexample to the abstract’s “allergenicity of cereals, legumes, and nuts decreases” generalization.
