# Potential impact of ultrasound, pulsed electric field, high-pressure processing and microfludization against thermal treatments preservation regarding sugarcane juice (*Saccharum officinarum*)

- **Citation (as given in the paper / filename):** Mukhtar K, Nabi BG, Arshad RN, Roobab U, Ranjha MMAN, Aadil RM, Ibrahim SA. Ultrason Sonochem. 2022;90:106194. doi:10.1016/j.ultsonch.2022.106194. PMID:36242792. PMC9576986.
- **Source file:** papers/2026-09-20-hpp-rerun/Mukhtar2022.txt
- **Extracted:** 2026-09-20
- **Screening:** Included (narrative review of thermal and nonthermal preservation of sugarcane juice, including HHP and high-pressure homogenization/microfluidization)

## Research question
How do thermal pasteurization, ohmic/microwave heating, and nonthermal HHP, ultrasound, PEF, UV, ozone, membranes, and microfluidization (high-pressure homogenization) change microbes, PPO/POD enzymes, and quality of sugarcane juice (SCJ), and which technology the authors rank as most used/efficient?

## Methods
Narrative review of published SCJ processing studies. Compiles process conditions and directional/numeric effects in Tables 1–3. No new experiment. Authors conclude among nonthermal methods HHP is the most utilized and efficient for SCJ, while ohmic heating is preferred among novel thermal methods because of less nutritional loss (Abstract; Highlights).

## Sample / data
Not a primary n. Endpoints come from cited SCJ trials (varieties, storage temperatures, and hurdle combinations as in the source papers).

## Claim-ready facts
- **Design:** narrative review of thermal, nonthermal, and hurdle processing of sugarcane juice
- **Population / model:** sugarcane juice (*Saccharum officinarum*); high sugar, pH typically 5.0–5.5, PPO/POD browning and fermentation (Introduction)
- **n:** not reported as a pooled sample; review of published SCJ trials
- **Intervention / comparator:** HHP (examples 300–600 MPa, sometimes with mild heat) and microfluidization/HPH (50–200 MPa, 1–7 cycles) versus thermal pasteurization, ohmic heating, microwave, US, PEF, UV, ozone, membranes, and combinations
- **Primary endpoint:** microbial log reductions (APC/TPC, yeast and mould, coliforms); PPO/POD residual activity; pH, TSS, TPC/TFC, vitamin C, colour
- **Primary result:** HHP examples compiled in Table 2: 300 MPa for 2 or 5 min maintained TSS, colour, and pH, with “less impact” on PPO (residual activity listed as 92.77) and a 6.32 log reduction in total plate count. HHP at 600 MPa/6 min/60 °C: greater POD than PPO reduction and significant microbial reduction (P < 0.05). HHP at 523 MPa/50 °C/11 min: total colour difference 2.8, total phenols 33 mg, antioxidant value 95%, 62% PPO inactivation, 59% POD inactivation, 5 log microbial reduction. Huang et al. as cited (Section 3.1): 200–600 MPa for 6 min; invertase on day 7 declined to 87.69% and 82.86%; APC fell 4.5 and 4.6 log CFU/mL at 400 and 600 MPa; yeast, mould, and coliforms below detection (<1.0 CFU/mL). Another cited HHP trial: 300 and 400 MPa for 10–25 min reduced 4.44, 5.31, and 6.30 log cycles of total bacteria, yeast and mould, and coliforms; 57% PPO inactivation at 400 MPa; no remarkable colour/pH effect (p > 0.05). Microfluidization/HPH is not equivalent to isostatic HHP: 100–140 MPa (1–2 cycles) decreased TSS 11% and cut bacteria 1.88 log; 50–200 MPa (1–7 cycles) *increased* PPO 39.4–64.7% and POD 16.4–75.0% in one study, while another reported 6 log CFU/mL bacterial reduction but 30–58% phenol loss at 100–150 MPa (Table 2; Section 3.2). Thermal pasteurization can inactivate PPO/POD completely at 95 °C/30 s but degrades nutrients (Section 2.3). Ohmic heating example: 32 V/cm, 1 min, up to 80 °C; PPO activity 10.07 ± 0.32%; vitamin C down ~33.3% immediately (Section 2.1).
- **Cannot show:** a new optimized SCJ HHP cycle from this paper; industrial cost/throughput (authors note most nonthermal methods remain laboratory-scale; Section 6); that HHP and microfluidization are interchangeable (they produce opposite enzyme trends in the compiled SCJ data).

## Key findings
- SCJ spoils by PPO browning and microbial fermentation of sucrose; thermal methods inactivate enzymes but harm nutrition and flavour (Introduction; Section 5).
- Authors rank HHP as the leading nonthermal SCJ option for retaining physicochemical parameters while reducing enzymes/microbes (Abstract; Section 3.1 conclusion).
- Hurdles (HHP + thermal 600 MPa/6 min with 97 °C/60 s; ozone + lactic acid; US + MW) are compiled as further microbial/enzyme reductions (Table 3).
- Limitations of HHP cited: equipment cost (Section 5).

## Limitations (as stated by the authors, or evident from the methods)
- Heterogeneous primary studies; some table cells mix residual activity percentages with log reductions and do not always report p-values or n.
- PEF, UV, and US data are included but are not HPP; use only as comparators.
- Microfluidization pressures (MPa through a homogenizer) are a different unit operation from isostatic HHP.

## Relevance to our research question
Sugarcane juice is a human food where heat is often *not* the desired kill step. Compiled HHP cycles (300 MPa/2–5 min versus 400 MPa versus 523 MPa/50 °C/11 min versus 600 MPa/6 min/60 °C) are not the same enzyme or log-reduction outcome. Closely related HPH/microfluidization can *increase* PPO/POD while HHP more often inactivates them.

## Open questions / things to verify
- Table 2 “PPO activity (92.77)” at 300 MPa is residual activity in the source, not 92.77% inactivation.
- “6.32 logs” at 300 MPa/2–5 min is strikingly high versus other 300 MPa juice literature and should be checked in the cited primary paper before pooling.
