# Ultra-high-pressure technology for preservation of fresh aquatic foods: A review

- **Citation (as given in the paper / filename):** Peng J, Liu C, Bai K, Xing S, Liu F. Czech J. Food Sci. 2023;41(5):313–322. doi:10.17221/87/2023-CJFS.
- **Source file:** papers/2026-09-20-hpp-rerun/Peng2023313.txt
- **Extracted:** 2026-09-20

## Research question
What do ultra-high-pressure (UHP / HHP / HPP) treatments do to microorganisms, enzymes, parasites, and quality of fresh aquatic foods, and what limits the technology?

## Methods
Narrative review. Web of Science and Scopus, English papers January 2012–March 2022, phrases for UHP/HHP/HPP plus pascalisation, enzymatic inactivation, and quality modification; cited references also screened. No original experiment.

## Sample / data
Secondary literature on fish, shrimp, shellfish, and related aquatic foods. Tables compile cited inactivation results (microorganisms Table 2; enzymes Table 3).

## Claim-ready facts
- **Design:** narrative review (2012–March 2022 literature)
- **Population / model:** fresh aquatic foods (fish, shrimp, shellfish, oysters, grass carp, etc.) as treated in the cited primary studies
- **n:** not applicable (review); search window stated, paper count not reported
- **Intervention / comparator:** UHP typically 100–1000 MPa via liquid medium vs untreated or thermal processes in the cited work
- **Primary endpoint:** microbial log reductions (CFU g^−1 or mL^−1), residual enzyme activity, parasite inactivation, and quality (texture, gel, colour) as reported by source papers
- **Primary result (as compiled, not generated here):** Oyster *Vibrio parahaemolyticus* fell from 10^9 to 10 CFU·mL^−1 at 350 MPa/14.5 min vs 500 MPa/30 s (Calik et al. 2002). Ye et al. 2015: 300 MPa/2 min at 0/25 °C reduced *V. parahaemolyticus* and *V. vulnificus* in *Crassostrea virginica* by >5 log CFU·g^−1 and completely inactivated them. Ginson et al. 2015 (*Fenneropenaeus indicus*, 250 MPa/6 min/25 °C): mesophiles −0.7, psychrophiles −1.2, proteolytic bacteria −1.2, Enterobacteriaceae −1.65, *Pseudomonas* −2.2, H2S producers −1.4, lactic acid bacteria −0.91 log CFU·g^−1. Phuvasate and Su 2015: 250 MPa/5 min/5 °C, *V. parahaemolyticus* in eastern oyster −6.2 log10 CFU·g^−1. Reyes et al. 2015 (*Trachurus murphyi*, 400 MPa/20 min/4 °C): psychrophiles −4.5, mesophiles −4.4, *Shewanella putrefaciens* −3.2 log CFU·g^−1. Bacterial spores “can withstand 1200 MPa at room temperature” (Simonin et al. 2012 cited). Black-tiger-shrimp PPO inactivation rose from 2% at 300 MPa/30 °C/3 min to 87% at 600 MPa/60 °C/15 min (Kaur and Rao 2017). Grass-carp calpain and myofibril-bound serine proteinases sharply reduced at ≤300 MPa/15 min/4 °C; collagenase, cathepsin B and D inhibited at ≥400 MPa; cathepsin L activated (Yu et al. 2018). Protein denaturation described as reversible at 100 MPa and irreversible above 200 MPa (Qi et al. 2015). *E. coli* pascalisation 2- to 6-fold more efficient at 50–60 °C than at 15–30 °C (Meng et al. 2016). Products look cooked at ≥650 MPa without a cooked flavour (Marangoni Júnior et al. 2019).
- **Cannot show:** a pooled safety equivalent of thermal pasteurisation for all aquatic foods; original spore-kill data; that one MPa–min cycle transfers across species.

## Key findings
- Mechanism (proposed/cited, not newly measured): UHP breaks non-covalent bonds, gels the phospholipid bilayer, ruptures vacuoles, and unfolds enzyme tertiary/quaternary structure while leaving peptide covalent bonds (Abstract; Enzyme inactivation section).
- Author interpretation: slightly lower pressure is required to inactivate parasites and viruses than bacteria (Aganovic et al. 2021 cited); named parasites include *Anisakis simplex* and *Clonorchis sinensis* metacercariae (Figure 3), without MPa–time numbers in the running text.
- EFSA is cited as concluding UHP presents no additional microbiological or chemical food-safety concern versus conventional treatments, while pathogen reductions in milk/colostrum under current industrial UHP are lower than legal thermal pasteurisation (Gomez-Estaca et al. 2018 as cited).

## Limitations (as stated by the authors, or evident from the methods)
- Review does not report how many papers were retrieved or screened.
- Common industrial doses do not inactivate all microorganisms, spores, or enzymes.
- Equipment cost and incomplete process-parameter tables for aquatic foods are named as barriers.
- Mechanism research is described as not deep enough to guide experiments.

## Relevance to our research question
Synthesises that UHP inactivates vegetative aquatic spoilage/pathogen bacteria in a pressure–time–temperature–matrix-dependent way, leaves spores standing at room-temperature megapascal levels, and that enzyme and cooked-appearance outcomes change with the named cycle (e.g., 250 vs 500 MPa; 300 vs 600 MPa plus heat). Directly supports “a named cycle is not the same outcome.”
