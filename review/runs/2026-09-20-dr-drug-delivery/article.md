# Ocular drug delivery for diabetic retinopathy: a narrative review of barriers, implants, and nanocarriers

## Abstract

Diabetic retinopathy damages retinal capillaries and, when fluid collects in the macula, produces diabetic macular edema, the leading cause of vision loss in working-age adults with diabetes. Licensed anti-vascular endothelial growth factor proteins and corticosteroids already work in the vitreous, yet they do so only after an injection, only for weeks or months, and only in a fraction of eyes that dry completely. Topical drops, which dominate marketed ocular products, rarely reach the posterior segment at useful concentrations because tear turnover and anterior barriers waste most of the dose. Delivery systems exist to change that geography: a biodegradable dexamethasone implant that peaks in two to three months, a fluocinolone implant labelled for years, a refillable ranibizumab port, and experimental nanoparticles meant to cross or bypass the same barriers. Human implant and port trials measure letters and macular thickness; rodent nanocarrier studies measure histology and leakage. Those endpoints are not interchangeable. The practical implication is to name the route, the duration of exposure, and the endpoint in the same sentence, and not to treat a rat retinal-thickness win as a clinic-ready drop.

## Keywords

diabetic retinopathy; diabetic macular edema; ocular drug delivery; dexamethasone implant; fluocinolone acetonide; Port Delivery System; nanoparticles; blood–retinal barrier

## Key Summary Points

- Conventional eye drops deliver little drug to the posterior segment; posterior diabetic disease is treated by injection or implant (review of anatomy and routes).
- Bolus anti-VEGF proteins improve vision in diabetic macular edema but have short intraocular residence and leave many maculas wet (clinical reviews of licensed injectables).
- A refillable ranibizumab port was noninferior to monthly injections for letters at one year in centre-involved edema, with more implant-related haemorrhage (phase 3 randomised trial).
- Dexamethasone implants reduce macular thickness more reliably than they raise letters in resistant disease, at the price of intraocular pressure and cataract (randomised subgroup plus observational series).
- Fluocinolone implants extend steroid exposure for years in already-treated edema; about one-third of real-world eyes still need extra injections (observational cohorts).
- Topical or intravitreal nanocarriers can change rodent posterior endpoints; a human diabetic-retinopathy nanocarrier trial is not among the included experiments (preclinical studies).

## 1. Introduction

Diabetic retinopathy is a microvascular disease of the inner retina. Persistent hyperglycaemia thickens capillary basement membranes, drops pericytes, and makes the inner blood–retinal barrier leak. Fluid that collects in the macula is diabetic macular edema (DME). That edema, more than peripheral ischaemic retina alone, is what most often costs working-age patients their driving vision. Laser photocoagulation can still close leaking microaneurysms and panretinal ischaemia, and vitrectomy can clear haemorrhage, but neither puts a drug at a molecular target for months at a time. The drugs that do—antibodies and traps against vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and corticosteroids—have to sit in the vitreous or in a device that empties into it.

The eye is a set of compartments, not a well-stirred tank, because tear film turns over in minutes, the cornea is a tight epithelium, and the conjunctiva drains into blood and lymph. The inner limiting membrane is a mesh at the vitreous–retina interface, and the inner blood–retinal barrier is endothelium with tight junctions, so a drop placed on the cornea has several chances to miss the macula. Reviews of ocular anatomy put topical bioavailability for conventional solutions below five percent, and they note that more than ninety-five percent of marketed ophthalmic products are still liquids meant for the anterior surface [1], [2]. Systemic tablets fare little better for the vitreous: only about one to two percent of a circulating small molecule is described as reaching that compartment, which is why oral fenofibrate and similar drugs are not a substitute for an intraocular injection when the endpoint is macular thickness [1].

What licensed therapy already does is therefore an injection into the vitreous. Ranibizumab, aflibercept, bevacizumab (off-label), and, more recently, faricimab bind VEGF or VEGF plus angiopoietin-2. They improve best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) and dry central subfield thickness (CST) in DME trials, and they are first-line in most algorithms [3], [4]. They also leave a large incomplete-response fraction: only about one-third to one-half of trial patients gain three lines, and persistent thickening after loading is common [4]. The proteins are cleared from the vitreous in days to a few weeks, so the clinic answer has been monthly or bimonthly needles [3], [5]. Each needle carries endophthalmitis risk, a visit, and a reason patients stop.

Corticosteroids are the other licensed intraocular class. They hit a broader inflammatory cascade than VEGF alone—cytokines, leukostasis, and tight-junction proteins as those pathways are reviewed clinically [4], [6]. The price is intraocular pressure (IOP) and cataract. A solution steroid would need the same frequent injections as an antibody. The formulation answer was to put dexamethasone in a biodegradable poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) rod that is labelled for about six months, and fluocinolone acetonide in a non-biodegradable polyimide/PVA implant that is labelled for about three years [7], [6], [8]. Those two devices are not interchangeable: they differ in polymer, daily dose, retreatment interval, and the IOP-monitoring calendar they force [9].

Nanocarriers are a third answer, still mostly preclinical. The hope is that a particle of tens to a few hundred nanometres can protect a small molecule or peptide, slow its release, and—if the coat is right—use mucus, CD44, or Müller-cell transcytosis to reach the outer retina without a monthly needle [10], [11], [12]. The risk is that the same particle can injure cornea, conjunctiva, or retina, depending on size, charge, and coat [11]. A particle that “arrives” on confocal microscopy has not yet lowered a letter score.

The live controversy is not whether anti-VEGF injections work. It is which failure of those injections a delivery system is actually solving. Duration of exposure is one failure: the protein is gone before the macula stays dry. Route is another: patients and surgeons would rather not enter the vitreous every month. Targeting is a third: a steroid implant treats the whole vitreous cavity, not a CD44-positive cell. Safety is a fourth: IOP after a steroid implant and vitreous haemorrhage after a surgical port are not the same adverse-event class as a missed anti-VEGF visit. Experimental nanocarriers and licensed implants are often listed in the same “future delivery” paragraph. They do not share an endpoint, a species, or a regulatory status, and treating them as one efficacy class would mix measurements the designs never placed on the same scale.

The aim of this review is to map how ocular delivery systems get anti-VEGF proteins, corticosteroids, and experimental payloads to the diabetic retina, and to keep duration, route, and translational limits in their own designs. The central argument of this review is that a carrier that arrives is not the same as a macula that dries, and that human implant and port trials cannot be pooled with rodent nanoparticle histology.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Search and sources

This is a narrative review of retrieved full texts, not a meta-analysis. Related journal articles were sought in OpenAlex as peer-reviewed papers from 2021 through 2026, using topic queries on drug delivery in diabetic retinopathy, ocular nanoparticles, and intravitreal implants for DME. Older full texts were added when a later comparison table left a numbered interpretation gap (dexamethasone-implant subgroup analysis, three-year fluocinolone real-world data, and Port Delivery System trials). Only public full texts were opened.

### 2.2 Eligibility

Inclusion required a readable full text about drug delivery (route, carrier, implant, formulation, or intraocular targeting) for diabetic retinopathy or DME, including original clinical, observational, and preclinical studies and narrative or consensus reviews that synthesise those tools. Exclusion: screening or pathogenesis papers with no delivery system; oral drugs without an ocular carrier; laser as the sole intervention; records without a public full text.

### 2.3 Study selection

Twenty-seven full texts were included. Designs range from phase 3 implant and port trials (or their published subgroups) through retrospective DME series to rodent nanoparticle experiments and secondary reviews. ## 3. Ocular barriers and routes that force a delivery system

### 3.1 Why a drop does not treat the macula

A topical drop is the default ophthalmic product because it is cheap and non-invasive. It is a poor posterior-segment product for the same reasons. Blinking (often cited as 6–15 times per minute) and tear turnover (about 0.5–2.2 µL/min) flush a solution that the conjunctival sac can hold only on the order of 30 µL [2], [13]. Corneal epithelial pores are about 2 nm, so most nanoparticles need a transcellular path rather than a pore [12]. The conjunctival surface area is far larger than the cornea, which sounds like a second door and is in practice a drain into blood [2], [12]. The net result, repeated across anatomy reviews, is topical bioavailability usually below five percent [1], [2]. Jacob and colleagues put conventional topical-solution bioavailability at five to ten percent while quoting the same blink and tear-turnover numbers [13]. Wang and colleagues state the clinical consequence for diabetic retinopathy directly: topical delivery is nearly ineffective for posterior-segment disease, which is why intraocular injection became standard despite its invasiveness [1].

Lipid nanoparticles for topical therapy (solid lipid nanoparticles, nanostructured lipid carriers, nanoemulsions, liposomes) are attempts to raise residence and trans-corneal flux for anterior disease and, in a few cited examples, to deepen penetration [13]. Those examples are mostly keratitis, glaucoma, or uveitis payloads. They teach the same barrier physics the diabetic retina faces, but they are not DME trials. Taken together, the drop problem is solved in clinic by abandoning the drop, not by a marketed posterior nanodrop.

### 3.2 Vitreous, inner limiting membrane, and nanoparticle filters

Once a particle is in the vitreous, a second set of filters appears. The inner limiting membrane has pores of about 10–25 nm and is a strict gate from vitreous to inner retina unless Müller cells transcytose the cargo [12]. In bovine vitreous, PEG-coated polystyrene beads that are charge-neutral can diffuse at sizes up to 750 nm, with better diffusion at 100–500 nm than at 750 nm; carboxylated 500 nm beads do not diffuse well, and positive polyethyleneimine particles do not diffuse after intravitreal injection [12]. Hyaluronic acid nanoparticles of 213.4 ± 10.3 nm (−26.2 ± 4.0 mV) and albumin nanoparticles of 326.3 ± 9.7 nm (−20.9 ± 2.0 mV) are reported to reach retina and cross the inner limiting membrane via Müller cells after injection [12]. Systemic gold particles crossed the inner blood–retinal barrier at 20 nm but not at 100 nm in the work those authors cite [12]. Charge, size, and coat are therefore not decorative. They decide whether a “retinal nanocarrier” is still in the vitreous gel.

Biodegradable polymers (PLGA, polylactic acid, polycaprolactone, hyaluronic acid, chitosan) are the usual materials for implants and particles meant to empty slowly [7]. Ozurdex is a PLGA dexamethasone rod that disappears; Iluvien is a non-biodegradable reservoir that does not [7], [8]. That material contrast is a duration decision, not a catalogue of every polysaccharide.

### 3.3 Why the vitreous injection exists

Periocular and suprachoroidal routes exist on paper as ways to avoid entering the vitreous [2], [3]. In the included full texts they remain maps, not new DME trials. Intravitreal injection exists because it puts a known milligram dose next to the macula, bypassing tears, cornea, and the outer blood–retinal barrier [3]. The cost is the needle frequency that later sections try to stretch. Taken together, sections 3.1–3.3 say that posterior diabetic drug delivery is an intraocular problem. This heading cannot show that a periocular hydrogel would fail in DME; it can show that the included evidence for treating DME is intraocular.

## 4. Bolus anti-VEGF proteins, durability engineering, and a refillable port

### 4.1 Short intraocular half-life and incomplete drying

Anti-VEGF proteins work. They also leave the eye. Kim and Woo summarise intraocular pharmacokinetics of marketed biologics and conclude that relatively short half-lives force monthly or bimonthly injections, with only a few extended-delivery techniques having reached trials despite many in vitro recipes [3]. Furino’s DME update puts numbers on incompleteness: only 33–45% of patients in large trials gain three lines, and in Protocol T (cited there) 31.6% (60/190) of aflibercept-treated eyes still had persistent DME through 24 weeks [4]. One-year CST reductions in that cited Protocol T split were 169 ± 138 µm (aflibercept), 147 ± 134 µm (ranibizumab), and 101 ± 121 µm (bevacizumab); letter gains depended on baseline vision, with a clinically relevant drug difference only in the worse-seeing band [4]. Those figures are secondary here. They fix the failure mode that implants and ports are asked to solve: not “anti-VEGF does not work,” but “anti-VEGF does not stay, and it does not dry everyone.”

### 4.2 Trap versus bispecific antibody as interval strategies

Aflibercept is a 115 kDa VEGF-A/B and placental growth factor trap; faricimab is a 150 kDa bispecific antibody against VEGF-A and angiopoietin-2 [5]. Liberski and colleagues did not run a new trial. They compiled YOSEMITE and RHINE (DME phase 3, cited): faricimab letter gains of about +10.7 to +11.8 versus aflibercept +10.3 to +10.9, with 51–53% of faricimab personalised-interval eyes reaching 16-week dosing, and CST reductions of about −187 to −207 µm versus aflibercept about −170 µm [5]. VIVID/VISTA week-52 aflibercept versus laser, also cited there, showed letter gains of +10.5 to +12.5 versus near zero for laser and CST reductions near −180 to −195 µm versus −66 to −73 µm [5]. Aflibercept vitreous half-life is cited as 7.1 days with clinical activity around 2.5 months [5]. Dual blockade is therefore a durability hypothesis measured in someone else’s randomised trials, not in this review’s laboratory. The authors themselves say superiority in anti-VEGF non-responders still has to be confirmed [5]. Kim quotes a different aflibercept molecular weight (~145 kDa) than Liberski (115 kDa) [3], [5]. That is a review discrepancy, not a new measurement; it does not change the interval problem.

### 4.3 Refillable ranibizumab port in edema and NPDR

The Port Delivery System (PDS) is a surgically placed, refillable reservoir that holds ranibizumab 100 mg/mL and is exchanged in clinic with a needle, not a new molecule [14]. Pagoda and Pavilion used the same device in different retinas, so their endpoints cannot be pooled. Pagoda randomised 634 adults with centre-involved DME (381 PDS every 24 weeks after four monthly 0.5 mg loading injections; 253 monthly 0.5 mg ranibizumab) at 87 United States sites [14]. Mean baseline BCVA was 65.3 ± 11.4 letters and CST 484.4 ± 128.7 µm; 77.6% had no prior anti-VEGF for DME [14]. Adjusted mean letter change averaged at weeks 60 and 64 was +9.6 versus +9.4 (difference 0.2; 95% CI −1.2 to 1.6), meeting a −4.5-letter noninferiority margin, and CST change was −203.5 versus −199.7 µm (difference −3.8; 95% CI −17.2 to 9.6) [14]. Supplemental ranibizumab was withheld in 95.9% and 97.4% of the two 24-week refill intervals [14]. Adverse events of special interest were 27.5% (88/320) versus 8.9% (28/314), including vitreous haemorrhage 9.7% versus 1.6%; letters fell 6.7 ± 12.0 at four weeks after implantation and recovered by 16 weeks while CST did not spike [14]. Both arms were still seen about every four weeks (15.6 versus 15.8 visits), so the trial does not show a visit-burden reduction [14]. Pavilion, by contrast, asked a severity-scale question in moderately severe to severe non-proliferative diabetic retinopathy without centre-involved DME, randomising 174 patients to PDS refilled every 36 weeks (n=106) versus monthly monitoring without PDS (n=68) [15]. At week 52, a two-step or greater Diabetic Retinopathy Severity Scale improvement occurred in 80.1% versus 9.0% (difference 71.1%; 95% CI 61.0 to 81.2; p<0.001), and composite incident centre-involved DME, proliferative disease, or anterior-segment neovascularisation was 7.1% versus 47.0% (hazard ratio 0.12; 95% CI 0.05 to 0.28) [15]. Letters at week 52 differed by 4.0 (95% CI 0.9 to 7.1) from a baseline already near 20/25; CST change was small (−18.8 versus +2.5 µm) because those maculas started dry, unlike Pagoda’s wet entry CST [15], [14]. Vitreous haemorrhage occurred in 5.7% of Pavilion PDS eyes, all within 28 days of implantation [15]. Pavilion is not a DME-drying trial; it is continuous ranibizumab against observation in a population whose maculas did not need drying. Primary endpoints for these human studies are summarised in Table 1 [14].

## 5. Biodegradable dexamethasone implants in diabetic macular edema

### 5.1 Release kinetics and randomised subgroup outcomes

Ozurdex is 0.7 mg dexamethasone in PLGA, delivered through a 22-gauge applicator, with a labelled biphasic release and a six-month retreatment floor in the registration programme [6], [7]. Kartı’s mini-review quotes MEAD (n=1048): ≥15-letter gains of 22.2% (0.7 mg), 18.4% (0.35 mg), and 12.0% sham, with mean central macular thickness (CMT) changes of −111.6 and −107.9 µm in the dexamethasone arms versus sham [6]. Taloni’s PRISMA-style narrative quotes the same MEAD letter and thickness figures and adds IOP-related adverse events of 36.0% / 34.1% / 5.1% and cataract-related events in phakic eyes of 67.9% / 64.1% / 20.4% [20]. The three-year Ophthalmology registration paper is cited here only through later reviews. The included MEAD analysis that can be checked independently is the previously treated subgroup: 247 dexamethasone 0.7 mg versus 261 sham, 70–75% of those arms [16]. In that subgroup, ≥15-letter gain at study end was 21.5% versus 11.1% (p=0.002); average BCVA change (area under the curve) +3.2 ± 8.7 versus +1.5 ± 7.5 letters; average CRT change −126 ± 131 versus −39 ± 121 µm [16]. Time to the tenth-percentile 15-letter response was 41 versus 184 days [16]. Prior anti-VEGF within that subgroup was only 25 versus 26 eyes, so this is not a head-to-head against continued antibody [16]. Mean treatments over three years were 4.1 versus 3.2, a six-month floor that the authors say may underestimate benefit relative to four- to five-month retreatment [16].

CHAMPLAIN, BEVORDEX, and Protocol U appear as quoted trials in Kartı and Taloni, not as independently checked primary sources [6], [20]. As quoted, CHAMPLAIN (vitrectomized DME) showed CMT −156 µm at month 2 fading to −39 µm at month 6; BEVORDEX showed greater CMT reduction with dexamethasone than bevacizumab without a two-year letter difference in pseudophakic eyes; Protocol U (dexamethasone added to ranibizumab for persistent DME) showed no letter difference at 24 weeks (+2.7 versus +3.0) with more anatomic drying (−110 versus −62 µm) [6], [20]. Those quoted rows explain why later observational papers treat anatomy and vision as split endpoints.

### 5.2 First-line versus switch, and real-world duration

Whether dexamethasone should be first-line or a switch is a guideline question more than a single-trial question. Spinetta compared seven national consensus documents: dexamethasone is endorsed first-line in vitrectomized eyes, recent cardiovascular events, poor anti-VEGF adherence, planned cataract surgery or pseudophakia, pregnancy in selected texts, and inflammatory optical-coherence-tomography signs (subretinal fluid, large empty cysts, hyperreflective foci, CMT >500 µm in one Asian panel) [21]. Switch rules differ: Italy after a failed anti-VEGF load; retreatment windows 3–5 months (France) or 4–6 months (Asia), with a fixed six-month wait described as inappropriate [21]. Taloni’s synthesis of first- versus second-line series found no systematic visual or CST advantage by line once indication mix is admitted, which is why those authors list niches rather than a universal first-line claim [20].

Neves and colleagues audited 57 Portuguese eyes treated with 0.7 mg dexamethasone because hospital anti-VEGF supply was interrupted, so the switch was not a protocol-defined failure cohort [19]. Mean implants over six months were 1.3. Letters peaked at month 3 (+8.64, p=0.041) and were no longer significant at month 6 (+3.43, p=0.147); CMT fell from week 2 through month 6 [19]. Naïve eyes (shorter DME duration: 6.2 versus 20.2 months) gained more letters than switch eyes (≥10-letter last observation 41.2% versus 10.5%) [19]. IOP rose +4.80 mmHg at month 1 and was managed topically; no cataract surgery occurred in six months, which is too short to compare with three-year MEAD cataract rates [19]. The pharmacokinetic reading the authors offer is that the labelled six-month reinjection should move toward about four months if the peak is month 2–3 [19].

Koç compared four retrospective arms after three monthly anti-VEGF injections left CMT >300 µm: continue aflibercept (n=25), continue ranibizumab (n=26), switch to dexamethasone after aflibercept (n=22), or switch after ranibizumab (n=21) [17]. From month 3 to month 6, CMT fell in every arm (for example 403.6 ± 70.4 to 291.6 ± 54.4 µm with continued aflibercept, p=0.000; 413.6 ± 88.1 to 320 ± 103.8 µm with aflibercept-then-dexamethasone, p=0.001) [17]. No arm had a significant within-group letter change; between-group month-6 BCVA p=0.159 [17]. IOP rose only in dexamethasone-switch groups (p=0.013 and 0.027) without medication or surgery [17]. Furino quotes a different retrospective switch (+6.1 ± 10.6 versus +0.4 ± 10.8 letters, p=0.004) that is not this cohort [4]. The disagreement is design and baseline: Koç’s dexamethasone arms entered month 3 with worse logMAR (0.80 and 0.85 versus 0.59 and 0.64) even though the Kruskal–Wallis p-value was 0.260 [17]. Anatomy improved either way; vision superiority of a switch is not established in Koç’s six-month series.

### 5.3 Vitrectomized eyes

Vitrectomy removes gel that slows drug clearance. Solution ranibizumab is expected to leave faster; a solid implant may not. Wang compared dexamethasone implant every 3–4 months (n=22) with treat-and-extend ranibizumab (n=26) in treatment-naïve, pseudophakic, previously vitrectomized DME [18]. At six months, dexamethasone outperformed ranibizumab on letters (final 0.38 ± 0.38 versus 0.62 ± 0.41 logMAR, p=0.04; ETDRS change +17.23 ± 15.32 versus +6.62 ± 14.02, p=0.03) and on central foveal thickness (−150.0 ± 131.1 versus −60.1 ± 110.2 µm, p=0.03), with fewer injections (2.6 ± 0.5 versus 5.6 ± 0.9) [18]. IOP-lowering drops were needed in 27.3% versus 0% [18]. The study is small, single-centre, and not randomised. It still names a condition cluster that solution-versus-implant comparisons in gel-filled eyes cannot substitute for. Taloni quotes Yuan and colleagues as finding no BCVA/CMT difference by vitrectomy status in aggregated DEX series, with duration 0.8 months shorter after vitrectomy [20]. Those two statements can both be true: a within-vitrectomized comparison (Wang) is not a vitrectomized-versus-not meta-analysis (Yuan, quoted).

### 5.4 Intraocular pressure and cataract as dexamethasone constraints

Goñi’s European consensus is written for both corticosteroid implants, but the dexamethasone-specific rates in MEAD and in Kartı’s safety compilation already warn that IOP is not a rare footnote [9], [6], [16]. In the previously treated MEAD subgroup, IOP-related adverse events were 38.1% versus 4.6% sham, and cataract-related events in baseline phakic eyes were 70.3% versus 20.1% [16]. Kartı compiles IOP rise ≥10 mmHg in 6.8–27.7% and IOP ≥25 mmHg across series, and lists glaucoma as a reason not to choose dexamethasone first [6]. Spinetta’s panels treat uncontrolled glaucoma as a contraindication and ask for IOP checks inside the first weeks [21]. Patil’s meta-analysis, as quoted by Taloni, found no cataract difference versus anti-VEGF, whereas He and colleagues reported relative risks of 2.68 for cataract and 4.14 for IOP-lowering therapy [20]. Follow-up length and baseline pseudophakia mix explain more of that spread than a single “steroids cause cataract” slogan. Taken together, dexamethasone buys months of exposure and anatomic drying; it does not buy a free letter, and it always buys an IOP calendar. This heading cannot show Protocol U’s add-on randomisation from a primary full text.

## 6. Longer-acting fluocinolone implants and dexamethasone-to-fluocinolone switches

### 6.1 Pharmacology and FAME as cited, not re-opened

Iluvien is 0.19 mg fluocinolone acetonide designed to release about 0.2 µg/day for up to 36 months [8], [7]. Fusi-Rubiano and colleagues, writing as a narrative review, quote FAMOUS aqueous pharmacokinetics (slightly more than 2 ng/ml for about three months, then 0.5–1.0 ng/ml through 36 months) and FAME ≥15-letter rates at month 24 of 28.7% (low-dose), 28.6% (high-dose), and 16.2% sham, with month-36 rates 28.7%, 27.8%, and 18.9% [8]. Chronic DME in FAME had a larger letter split (34.0% versus 13.4% sham at month 36 for low-dose) than non-chronic disease [8], [4]. Cataract by month 36 was 81.7% (low-dose) versus 50.7% sham; IOP-lowering drops 38.4% versus 14.1%; incisional IOP surgery 4.8% versus 0.5% at 36 months for low-dose [8]. Those FAME numbers are cited secondary evidence. The three-year Ophthalmology fluocinolone registration paper is not an independent primary source in this review.

### 6.2 Real-world three-year and switch cohorts

Retro-IDEAL followed 81 German DME eyes (63 patients) after 0.19 mg fluocinolone for a mean 30.8 ± 11.0 months [22]. Baseline letters were 49.0; peak gain was +5.5 letters at month 9 (n=56), maintained to month 30 (n=42); year-3 mean letters were 52.4 [22]. CMT fell from 502 µm to 338 µm at year 1 (n=43) and 318 µm at year 3 (n=29), a mean change of −158 µm at 36 months [22]. Supplemental anti-VEGF or steroid was used in 25/81 (30.9%) [22]. IOP-lowering treatment rose from 22.2% before to 27.2% after; surgery 3.7% [22]. That is a three-year real-world duration signal, unlike the sham-controlled FAME rates quoted by Fusi-Rubiano [22], [8].

REALFAc enrolled 62 French eyes (46 patients) already treated with dexamethasone, with mean follow-up 13.9 ± 7.5 months [23]. Baseline BCVA was already 64.0 ± 17.2 letters and CMT 333.3 ± 112.5 µm—a quieter macula than Retro-IDEAL’s 502 µm [23], [22]. Peak mean letters were 66.8 ± 16.7 at three months (p=0.03); maximum gain +5.0 ± 12.7 letters at 21 months; ≥15-letter responders 19.4% [23]. Best CMT was 280.5 ± 74.4 µm at 18 months (p<0.0001) [23]. Additional DME treatment occurred in 23/62 (37.1%), stretching injection intervals from every 3.7 ± 9.9 months before fluocinolone to every 10.3 ± 5.8 months after [23]. Ocular hypertension was recorded in 11.3%; an IOP-lowering procedure in 17.7% [23]. Both phakic eyes had cataract surgery at days 250 and 251 [23].

Baillif and colleagues switched 113 eyes from dexamethasone to fluocinolone at 30 French sites, mean follow-up 7.6 months [24]. Baseline letters 54.1 ± 17.8 improved to 59.8 at month 4 (n=51); peak gain +5.3 letters; CMT 454.7 ± 196.7 µm fell to 320.9 µm at month 3 [24]. Additional treatment after fluocinolone occurred in 37/113 (32.7%) [24]. IOP >21 / >25 / >30 mmHg occurred in 15.9% / 10.6% / 3.5%; mean IOP stayed below 19 mmHg at visits [24]. Seventy-five of 102 eyes (73.5%) were switched within eight weeks of last dexamethasone; longer gaps associated with higher baseline CMT and more additional treatment [24]. Letter gains of about five in Baillif, Mathis, and Augustin look similar and are not the same population: entry CMT 455 versus 333 versus 502 µm, follow-up 7.6 versus 13.9 versus 31 months, and different prior control [24], [23], [22]. Fusi-Rubiano devote a heading to vitrectomized eyes because solution pharmacokinetics change after gel removal whereas a reservoir implant may not [8]. The series they summarise are small and uncontrolled relative to Wang’s dexamethasone-versus-ranibizumab comparison in vitrectomized DME [8], [18]. Pagoda’s gel-filled PDS eyes, Koç’s non-vitrectomized switches, and fluocinolone after vitrectomy are therefore not one duration class.

### 6.3 Intraocular pressure monitoring after fluocinolone

Goñi and colleagues convened glaucoma and retina specialists to set IOP visit frequency after corticosteroid implants [9]. Standard checks at 2–7 days, one month, and two or three months, then every three months for low-risk eyes for the implant’s life, are the backbone [9]. High-risk eyes (prior IOP events) are treated within one to two weeks if pressure is high and referred if still >25 mmHg after two treatments [9]. Compiled cited rates, which they refuse to pool, include IOP ≥21 mmHg in 31–60% of patients/eyes across studies, ≥30 mmHg in 5–18%, and medication in up to 46% [9]. A cited Medisoft analysis they quote showed prior IOP events raising later ≥10 mmHg rises (45.7% versus 19.1%) [9]. Europe lists advanced glaucoma not controlled by medicines as a contraindication to Ozurdex [9]. The monitoring paper does not enrol a new cohort. It is the translational constraint on the three-year device: you cannot have 36-month steroid exposure without 36-month glaucoma logistics. ## 7. Experimental nanocarriers aimed at the diabetic retina

### 7.1 Topical hyaluronic-acid albumin versus injected PEDF particles

Radwan and colleagues loaded apatinib, a VEGFR-2 inhibitor, into bovine-serum-albumin nanoparticles and coated them with hyaluronic acid for topical use in streptozotocin diabetic rats [25]. Selected coated particles were 222.2 ± 3.56 nm, zeta −37.3 ± 1.8 mV, encapsulation 69 ± 1%; uncoated particles were 198.9 ± 3.52 nm and −29.2 ± 1.3 mV [25]. Twenty-four-hour release was 66.67% coated versus 98% uncoated [25]. Efficacy groups had n=5. Untreated diabetic retinal thickness was 220.24 µm versus 151.84 µm in normals; topical coated nanoparticles reached 158.97 µm (not different from normal at p>0.05), and hyaluronic-acid-coated groups reached basement-membrane thickness 0.09 µm, comparable to controls [25]. In diabetic eyes, topical coated versus uncoated fluorescence 5 h after a single drop was 12.28 ± 1.39 versus 3.55 ± 0.81; the coated topical signal was 44% of a single intravitreal coated dose [25]. Volumes were 50 µL twice daily for 14 days versus a single 10 µL injection; milligrams of apatinib per eye are not stated [25]. CD44 uptake is proposed from prior literature and from the fluorescence difference; occupancy was not measured [25]. Rabbit corneal epithelial viability stayed above 95% after 30 minutes [25]. That is histology in a rat after two weeks of drops, not ETDRS letters.

Qu, by contrast, encapsulated a 34-amino-acid N-terminal pigment-epithelium-derived-factor peptide in PLGA and injected it once into oxygen-induced retinopathy mice and streptozotocin rats—an intravitreal depot rather than a drop, and a peptide rather than a VEGFR-2 small molecule [26], [25]. In hypoxic ARPE19 and retinal endothelial cells the particles reduced VEGF protein without dropping ARPE19 viability [26]. In oxygen-induced retinopathy, non-perfusion and pre-retinal nuclei fell versus empty particles (n=6; percentages not printed) [26]. In diabetic rats four weeks after injection, Evans-blue leakage was lower than with empty particles (n=5), with reductions in retinal albumin, VEGF, TNF-α, and ICAM-1 on small Western blots (n=3) [26]. Methods state 20 µg/eye; figure legends state 5 µg/eye—an internal conflict that blocks a dose claim [26]. The two experiments share a streptozotocin retina and a nanoparticle size class near 200 nm, yet they differ in route, payload, species mix, and endpoint (thickness and basement membrane versus Evans-blue leakage), so they cannot be ranked as one “nanocarrier win” [25], [26].

### 7.2 Ocuserts without a retinal endpoint, and toxicity as a constraint

Ramadan optimised vildagliptin solid-lipid nanoparticles in dual-coated ocuserts for once-daily-intended ocular residence [27]. Selected solid-lipid particles were 137.8 ± 2.43 nm, zeta −18.1 ± 1.60 mV, encapsulation 43.44 ± 3.17% [27]. In vivo remaining-drug release in rabbits was 35.12 ± 2.47% at 24 h (n=3 at that time point of 24 rabbits) [27]. Draize irritation scores were 0 after day 1 [27]. The abstract frames diabetic retinopathy, whereas the experiment does not measure retinal thickness, leakage, or VEGF, unlike Radwan and Qu [27], [25], [26]. Liu’s and Wang’s nanotechnology reviews list many other cited rodent particles (gold, liposomes, dendrimers, siRNA carriers) as a map of what people have tried [10], [1]. Those cited recipes are not new n here. Ramadan is an original formulation study with a diabetic-retinopathy framing; it cannot join Radwan and Qu in an efficacy cluster.

Cao and colleagues review ocular nanoparticle applications and then the ways common particles can injure cornea, conjunctiva, and retina depending on route, size, shape, charge, and coat [11]. Their synthesis is that nano-delivery systems are generally described as promising in animal toxicology and still need human-relevant assays, including organoids [11]. Jacob’s topical lipid-nanoparticle review is mostly anterior-segment; it does not cancel Cao’s warning for posterior particles [13]. Swetledge’s distribution physics (section 3.2) is the mechanistic reason a toxicology paper cannot be ignored: if a 200 nm cationic particle never leaves the vitreous, its retinal toxicity profile is a different experiment from a 20 nm gold particle that crossed the inner blood–retinal barrier in a cited study [12], [11]. ## 8. Discussion

The included literature supports a short causal chain. Diabetic inner retina leaks and ischaemic retina drives VEGF. A drop does not reach that retina at licensed bioavailability, so therapy is intraocular [1], [2]. A protein in solution dries many maculas and leaves many others wet, because it is cleared in days to weeks [3], [4]. A biodegradable dexamethasone rod stretches exposure to months and dries anatomy more reliably than it raises letters in resistant six-month series [16], [17], [19]. A fluocinolone reservoir stretches exposure to years in already-treated eyes, with letter gains of about five in real-world cohorts that often start with better vision than registration DME [23], [24], [22]. A refillable ranibizumab port matches monthly injections on letters in DME and prevents incident edema in non-proliferative disease without DME, at a surgical adverse-event cost [14], [15]. A topical albumin nanoparticle can normalise rat retinal thickness; that is a different species and a different endpoint [25].

Those sentences are not a ranking of “best delivery system.” They are what each cluster measured. Pooling Pagoda’s +9.6 letters with Radwan’s 158.97 µm would be a unit error. Pooling Koç’s non-significant six-month letter change with MEAD’s 21.5% 15-letter rate would ignore naïve versus resistant disease, three years versus six months, and sham versus continued anti-VEGF [16], [17]. Pooling Baillif’s +5.3 letters with Retro-IDEAL’s +5.5 letters without naming entry CMT invents precision [24], [22]. Goñi’s refusal to pool IOP rates across implant studies is the right methodological instinct for letters as well [9].

Mechanism grades should stay honest. Hyaluronic-acid–CD44 targeting in Radwan is proposed [25]. PEDF34’s anti-VEGF effect in Qu is measured as protein and leakage, not as a receptor occupancy study [26]. Faricimab’s angiopoietin-2 arm is a cited trial story [5]. PLGA hydrolysis of Ozurdex and the 0.2 µg/day fluocinolone release curve are labelled device pharmacology, measured in FAMOUS as quoted [7], [8]. Postoperative vitreous haemorrhage after PDS is a measured surgical event, not a VEGF-biology event [14], [15].

What remains open is still large. The three-year MEAD and FAME registration papers and Protocol U are cited here only through later reviews; quoted numbers are labelled secondary [6], [20], [8], [4]. A human nanocarrier trial in diabetic retinopathy is not among the included experiments. Microneedles, iontophoresis, and suprachoroidal cannulas appear in maps and not as primary DME experiments here [2], [3], [7]. Head-to-head randomisation of dexamethasone versus continued anti-VEGF after incomplete loading is not Koç’s retrospective four-arm study [17]. Pagoda does not show fewer visits [14]. Apatinib micrograms per drop are unknown [25].

Clinically, the delivery choice is already a constraint choice. Eyes that cannot absorb monthly injections, that have inflammatory optical-coherence-tomography signs, that are vitrectomized, or that have recent arterial events are the niches in which dexamethasone consensuses step in front of anti-VEGF [21], [20]. Eyes that have already used dexamethasone and still need years of drying are the fluocinolone niche, provided glaucoma logistics exist [23], [9]. Eyes that can undergo implant surgery and accept a haemorrhage rate can match monthly ranibizumab with a 24-week refill [14]. Eyes that are still on a drop bottle for posterior disease are on a route the anatomy reviews say should not work, unless a particle as specific as Radwan’s coated albumin—and a human trial that is not yet among the included experiments—changes that rule [1], [25]. Taken together, the delivery choice is a constraint choice: duration, route, targeting, and adverse-event class are not one ranking.

## 9. Conclusions

Posterior diabetic retinopathy is treated in the vitreous because the ocular surface wastes drops. Licensed anti-VEGF proteins remain the visual-acuity backbone and remain short-acting. Dexamethasone implants trade injection frequency for pressure and cataract and for an anatomic effect that is clearer than a letter effect in resistant six-month data. Fluocinolone implants extend that steroid logic to years in chronic, previously treated edema, with about one-third of real-world eyes still needing extra injections. A refillable ranibizumab port can match monthly injections on letters in centre-involved edema if surgical adverse events are acceptable. Experimental nanocarriers can change rodent posterior histology and leakage; they have not, in the included experiments, changed a human letter score. The next measurements that would change practice are independent checking of the steroid registration trials still cited only secondarily, a randomised switch after incomplete anti-VEGF loading, visit-burden outcomes for ports in ordinary clinics, and a first-in-human posterior nanocarrier study that reports both retinal drug and ETDRS letters rather than fluorescence alone. Overall, a carrier that arrives is not the same as a macula that dries.
