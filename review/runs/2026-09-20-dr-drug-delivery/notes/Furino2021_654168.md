# Intravitreal Therapy for Diabetic Macular Edema: An Update

- **Citation (as given in the paper / filename):** Furino C, Boscia F, Reibaldi M, Alessio G. J Ophthalmol. 2021;2021:6654168. doi:10.1155/2021/6654168
- **Source file:** papers/2026-09-20-dr-drug-delivery-found/Furino2021_654168.txt
- **Extracted:** 2026-09-20
- **Screening:** Included (narrative review of anti-VEGF and sustained-release corticosteroids for DME).

## Research question
What is the role of anti-VEGF agents and intravitreal sustained-release corticosteroid devices in DME, and does switching from anti-VEGF to a dexamethasone (DEX) implant help eyes with a suboptimal anti-VEGF response?

## Methods
Narrative review (not a systematic review or new trial). Authors synthesise RCTs and selected real-world studies of pegaptanib, bevacizumab, ranibizumab, aflibercept, DEX implant, and fluocinolone acetonide implants, plus switching studies. Pathophysiology is summarised from cited work (Daruich; Romero-Aroca). No search protocol, inclusion dates, or risk-of-bias tool is reported.

## Sample / data
No new patient cohort. Cited trial sizes include Protocol T (aflibercept/bevacizumab/ranibizumab; 1-year VA table n≈102–106 per arm in the <69-letter stratum and ≈104–106 in the 69–78-letter stratum), MEAD (DEX 0.7/0.35 mg vs sham; 1,048 randomised in a pooled anatomic analysis, 607 completing all visits), BEVORDEX (88 eyes/61 patients), PLACID (253 patients), FAME (low-dose 0.2 µg/day vs high-dose 0.5 µg/day vs sham), and a retrospective switch study (110 eyes: 72 continued anti-VEGF, 38 switched to DEX). A cited claims analysis reported treatment within 28 days of new DME diagnosis among 13,410 treatment-naïve patients.

## Claim-ready facts

- **Design:** narrative review of published RCTs and observational DME studies (this paper did not enrol a new sample)
- **Population / model:** patients with DME in cited trials and real-world series (centre-involving DME for anti-VEGF first-line discussion)
- **n:** not applicable for this paper as an experiment; largest cited DME trials include Protocol T strata (~100 eyes/arm/VA band at 1 year) and MEAD ITT n=1,048
- **Intervention / comparator:** cited anti-VEGFs (bevacizumab 1.25 mg, ranibizumab 0.3 mg, aflibercept 2.0 mg in Protocol T) vs one another; DEX 0.7 mg implant vs sham/laser/bevacizumab; FAc 0.2 µg/day (approved Iluvien 0.19 mg) vs sham; early switch to DEX vs continued anti-VEGF
- **Primary endpoint:** as in the cited trials, typically BCVA letter change or ≥10/≥15-letter gain and CRT/CMT (µm); this review’s stated aim is comparative role and switching convenience
- **Primary result (this paper’s synthesis, numbers from cited trials — do not treat as Furino’s own measurements):** Anti-VEGF first-line; only 33–45% of DME patients in large trials gain ≥3 lines (Introduction/Treatment Strategies). Protocol T 1-year CST reduction: aflibercept 169 ± 138 µm, ranibizumab 147 ± 134 µm, bevacizumab 101 ± 121 µm. In eyes with baseline VA <69 letters, 1-year mean VA gain 18.9 (aflibercept), 11.8 (bevacizumab), 14.2 (ranibizumab) letters; in eyes with 69–78 letters, gains 8.0, 7.5, and 8.3 letters (no clinically relevant drug difference). Persistent DME through 24 weeks still in 31.6% (60/190) of aflibercept eyes. MEAD year-3 ≥15-letter gain: 22.2% (DEX 0.7 mg) and 18.4% (0.35 mg) vs sham; CMT −111.6 ± 134.1 µm (0.7 mg) vs −41.9 ± 116.0 µm sham. BEVORDEX month-12 ≥10-letter gain 41% DEX vs 40% bevacizumab (p=0.83) with fewer injections (2.7 vs 8.6) and greater CMT reduction (187 vs 122 µm, p=0.015). FAME month-24 mean BCVA gain 4.4 / 5.4 / 1.7 letters (low-dose / high-dose / sham); glaucoma surgery 3.7% / 7.6% / 0.5%. Chronic DME (≥3 years) FAME month-36 ≥15-letter gain 34.0% vs sham 13.4% (p<0.001); nonchronic 22.3% vs 27.8% (p=0.275). Retrospective switch after 3 anti-VEGF: DEX +6.1 ± 10.6 vs continued anti-VEGF +0.4 ± 10.8 letters (p=0.004); CMT −92.8 ± 173.6 vs +18.3 ± 145.9 µm (p<0.001). (Abstract; §§3.1–3.2)
- **Cannot show:** a new head-to-head RCT; nanoparticle/hydrogel/microneedle delivery; pharmacokinetics measured by these authors; causal proof that early DEX switch is superior (switch evidence is largely retrospective).

## Key findings

- Authors’ clinical conclusion (Abstract): choose anti-VEGF by baseline BCVA — aflibercept preferred when baseline BCVA is low; bevacizumab, ranibizumab, and aflibercept similar when baseline BCVA is higher. DEX implants are usually second-line; switching after ~3 monthly anti-VEGF injections may give better function than persisting with anti-VEGF.
- Treatment-pattern citation: of 13,410 treatment-naïve DME diagnoses, within 28 days 74.5% observation, 15.6% anti-VEGF, 8.4% laser, 1.0% corticosteroids, 0.5% combination (Introduction).
- DEX device (cited): 0.7 mg micronized dexamethasone in biodegradable PLGA, release ~6 months; FDA/Europe approval 2014 on MEAD (Section 3.2.1).
- MEAD safety (cited): cataract-related events in phakic eyes 67.9% / 64.1% / 20.4% (0.7 mg / 0.35 mg / sham); cataract surgery 59.2% / 52.3% / 7.2%; IOP increase 27.7% / 24.8% / 3.7%; glaucoma procedure 1.4% / 0.9% / 0.3%.
- Iluvien approved load 0.19 mg, labelled 0.2 µg/day over 36 months; the review states pharmacokinetic studies reported sustained delivery for approximately one year (Section 3.2.2 — cited PK, not measured here).
- Mechanism statements are cited/proposed: hyperglycemia, hypoxia, leukostasis, IL-6/IL-8/IL-1β/VEGF/TNF-α, BRB disruption (Pathophysiology). Corticosteroids said to inhibit inflammatory processes and VEGF and to stabilise capillaries (Section 3.2) — not newly assayed in this paper.
- Authors’ positioning (Conclusions): corticosteroids first-line in patients at cardiovascular/cerebrovascular risk or unwilling to accept monthly anti-VEGF; use DEX before FAc; reserve triamcinolone when approved agents are unavailable.

## Limitations (as stated by the authors, or evident from the methods)

- Not a systematic review; no methods for study selection or quality.
- Switching recommendation rests on retrospective series plus the authors’ reading of Protocol I post hoc (<5-letter gain at week 12 in 40% of eyes — cited).
- Many tabulated comparator numbers come from heterogeneous trials (laser vs sham vs other anti-VEGF).
- No original safety follow-up.

## Relevance to our research question
Maps how current intravitreal anti-VEGF and corticosteroid implants actually reach the macula in DME (bolus vs 6-month vs ~3-year release), why duration and incomplete VEGF response still limit outcomes, and where a DEX switch is argued. Does not cover nanocarriers.

## Open questions / things to verify

- All numeric efficacy/safety figures are from cited trials; do not write “Furino et al. found 22.2%…” as if REALFAc/MEAD were this team’s experiment.
- Protocol T ranibizumab dose in the US trial was 0.3 mg; European practice often uses 0.5 mg — the review reports Protocol T’s 0.3 mg.
- PK “approximately one year” for FAc contradicts the 36-month label and other papers in this sample (Baillif/Mathis); keep as this review’s cited statement.
