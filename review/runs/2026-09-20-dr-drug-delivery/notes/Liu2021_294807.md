# Progress of Nanotechnology in Diabetic Retinopathy Treatment

- **Citation (as given in the paper / filename):** Liu Y, Wu N. Int J Nanomedicine. 2021;16:1391–1403. doi:10.2147/IJN.S294807
- **Source file:** papers/2026-09-20-dr-drug-delivery-found/Liu2021_294807.txt
- **Extracted:** 2026-09-20
- **Screening:** Included (narrative review of nanotechnology for DR diagnosis, delivery, and retinal regeneration)

## Research question
How has nanotechnology been applied to prevent, diagnose, deliver drugs through the blood–retinal barrier, and support neural regeneration in diabetic retinopathy, relative to laser, vitrectomy, and conventional drugs?

## Methods
Narrative review. After background on nanotechnology (0.1–100 nm) and diabetes complications, the authors review traditional DR therapies, then nanotechnology for fundus imaging, hypoxia targeting, drug delivery, monitoring, and retinal regeneration scaffolds/gene carriers (Abstract; body headings through Conclusions). No systematic search or included-study count.

## Sample / data
Secondary literature plus a summary table of nanomaterials used in DR (Table 1). Traditional-therapy numbers are quoted from cited clinical series. Nanocarrier results are quoted from cited animal/in vitro papers.

## Claim-ready facts
- **Design:** narrative review of nanotechnology in DR treatment.
- **Population / model:** clinical DR therapies as described in cited series; nanocarrier work in diabetic rabbits/rats/mice, HRECs, retinal explants, and cell-culture compatibility assays as quoted.
- **n:** not reported (review). Cited series that the review does quote include Jiang et al. 45 patients (PRP); Kleinmann et al. follow-up of 192 laser-treated patients; Naruse/Veritti et al. 185 cases of 25G or 27G vitrectomy (64 of 25G and 125 of 27G).
- **Intervention / comparator:** laser photocoagulation, vitrectomy, periocular/subretinal/intravitreal conventional drugs (anti-VEGF, steroids, microcirculation agents) versus nanoparticle carriers, imaging NPs, nanoscaffolds, and gene nanocarriers.
- **Primary endpoint:** not applicable as original data; review endpoints are BRB penetration, sustained retinal presence, anti-angiogenic/anti-inflammatory effect, imaging utility, and scaffold-guided axon growth as reported by cited studies.
- **Primary result:** Clinical laser, anti-VEGF injection, and vitrectomy are widely used, but drugs have low bioavailability, poor BRB penetrability, toxicity, and enzymatic degradation that shorten duration; nanotechnology is reviewed as offering small diameter, high BRB penetrability, biocompatibility, sustained release, and possible targeting of RPE receptors (Abstract; Traditional Therapies; Application Progress).
- **Cannot show:** human safety of the named nanocarriers; a preferred NP chemistry for DR; original comparative trials.
- **Mechanism (cited / proposed):** Most nanomolecules used as carriers are described as 1–100 nm so they “easily pass through the BRB”; surface binding to specific receptors on RPE cells is proposed as the targeting mechanism (Progress of Nanotechnology in DR Drug Treatment). GLUT1/GLUT3 changes in diabetic retina are cited as the rationale for HA nanocarriers aimed at CD44 on RPE (cited Badr, Ulas, Gao).
- **Traditional-therapy numbers the review quotes:** delayed photocoagulation associated with 6.5% serious vision loss vs timely PRP 3.8%–4.7% (Bi et al.); Kleinmann et al. 39% vitreous haemorrhage among 192 laser-treated patients; laser wavelengths commonly 532 nm and 577 nm; Nathan et al. early vitrectomy in Type I diabetes: visual acuity 10/20 or better in 36% of eyes vs 12% with deferral, with no advantage of early vitrectomy in Type 2 (Traditional Therapies).

## Key findings
- International Diabetes Alliance figures quoted: 400 million people with diabetes in 2015, 5 million diabetes deaths, total expected 600 million by 2040 (Diabetes and Complications).
- Anti-VEGF drugs “commonly used in DR treatment” are listed as ranibizumab, bevacizumab, and infliximab; “for moderate to severe DR, infliximab shows better visual recovery efficacy” (Anti-VEGF Drugs, citing reference 34). Infliximab is an anti-TNF agent — record as the review’s wording.
- Steroids named: triamcinolone acetonide (“most effective steroid drug used for PDR,” discovered in 1970), dexamethasone sodium phosphate, and fluocinolone acetate injected into the vitreous; “improvement of the bioavailability and the targeting of these drugs to the retina are critical challenges” (Steroid Hormones).
- Imaging: PEI-NHAc-FS nanoparticles cited as reducing fluorescein sodium retention while developing retinal vessels (Qian et al.); vanadium core-shell nanorods cited for plasma metabolic fingerprints and NPDR grading, “applied in clinical trials” (review’s wording).
- Hypoxia targeting: Iwase et al. PSA-PEG nanoparticles continuously delivered daunorubicin after intraocular injection in a diabetic rabbit model — antagonist released in a controlled manner for more than 105 days; PSA-PEG NPs continuously inhibit retinal neovascularization for more than 35 days and are described as non-toxic to the rabbit retina; human retinal safety “no conclusion has yet been reached” (Application Progress).
- Gao et al.: HA nanocarriers targeting CD44 of RPE, GLUT1 coated, injected into subretinal space of diabetic mice; review states they “significantly control the blood sugar level in the retina”; GENPs called safe/non-toxic based on outer nuclear layer thickness (cited).
- Hao et al.: PCL-PF68 NPs as HA (steroid) eye drops in diabetic rats; after 24 h exposure “cell survival rates of NPBs and NPDs are both 100%”; limitations as a lipid carrier include low drug loading and poor storage stability.
- Amadio et al. 2016: SLN/liposome siRNA silencing HuR in STZ rats — nanocarriers carried miRNA/siRNA through the BRB (cited).
- Zamani et al.: mPEG-PCL micelles/α-CD hydrogel — review quotes 70% of Avastin released within 5 days and 70% of Dex within 1 day (corneal NV rat model; the review then discusses retinal NV — keep as quoted).
- Amato et al. 2020: magnetic NP–octreotide; significant effect maintained at lower doses (100-fold in HRECs and 10-fold in retinal explants) vs free OCT; release in rat retina “for several months” without toxicity (cited).
- Shoval et al. 2019: carbon nanodot-coated bevacizumab and aflibercept in diabetic rats — fluorescence used for non-invasive concentration monitoring; C-dots described as preventing degradation before the retina (cited).
- Table 1 (as printed): CNE 2014; SLN/NLC 2013, diameter 288 nm, triamcinolone acetonide, PEG loading improves ocular bioavailability but low loading/poor storage; SiO2–CeCl3 130 nm (2014); AgNPS 35 nm (2019) with genotoxicity via ROS/DNA damage; GNP 50 nm (2011), no retinal toxicity found but cytotoxicity “may occur as the particle size shrinks”; MNP 50 nm (2020) with octreotide; PLGA 20–250 nm (2012/2013) protects protein but poor water solubility and hepatic/splenic clearance; chitosan-based NPs (2014) sustain bevacizumab in the retina.
- Regeneration: radiating nanofiber scaffold — 72% of nerve axons grew along the scaffold (Milton Wyman, cited); nanofibers ~200 nm mimic nerve-fiber distribution better than ~1000 nm (cited).

## Limitations (as stated by the authors, or evident from the methods)
- “Safety and feasibility of those new nanomaterials are still in the research stage” (Introduction to nanotechnology in DR).
- Before clinical trial application, safety, PK, and PD of each nanocarrier “shall be treated with caution” (Conclusions).
- Table 1 lists “existing problems” for several materials (storage instability, genotoxicity, liver/spleen clearance).
- Review mixes DR delivery with diabetic foot, neuropathy, and nephropathy background.
- Not a systematic evidence synthesis.

## Relevance to our research question
Directly on nanoparticle routes to the diabetic retina (BRB crossing, RPE targeting, anti-VEGF/steroid/siRNA payloads) and on what still blocks translation (human safety, loading, clearance).

## Open questions / things to verify
- Infliximab classified as an “anti-VEGF drug” for DR is likely a mis-categorisation; keep as the review’s claim and do not upgrade it.
- Zamani hydrogel data are from inflammatory corneal neovascularization; the review applies them to retinal NV.
- Table 1 diameters and years are as printed; several “N/A” cells.
- GLUT1 expression: the review juxtaposes Badr (reduced GLUT1) and Ulas (reduced ~50% yet also “elevated GLUT1 and GLUT3”) — internally tense; do not pick one without the primary papers.
