# Recent advances in the treatment and delivery system of diabetic retinopathy

- **Citation (as given in the paper / filename):** Wang Z, Zhang N, Lin P, Xing Y, Yang N. Front Endocrinol. 2024;15:1347864. doi:10.3389/fendo.2024.1347864
- **Source file:** papers/2026-09-20-dr-drug-delivery-found/Wang2024_347864.txt
- **Extracted:** 2026-09-20
- **Screening:** Included (DR-focused narrative review covering anti-VEGF, steroids, and nanocarrier delivery)

## Research question
What is the current pathophysiology and standard treatment of diabetic retinopathy (DR), and how might novel receptors, gene therapy, and nanocarrier delivery systems overcome the limits of laser, vitrectomy, and repeated intravitreal injections?

## Methods
Narrative review (article type: Review). Sections cover DR pathophysiology, laser and surgery, anti-VEGF and steroid drugs, gene therapy, ocular absorption barriers, and nanocarriers (nanoliposomes, polymeric and lipid nanoparticles, nanoemulsions, nanogels, dendrimers) (Abstract; Sections 1–5). No systematic search protocol or included-study count is reported.

## Sample / data
Secondary literature on DR epidemiology, clinical anti-VEGF/steroid use, and mostly preclinical nanocarrier studies in diabetic rodents. Epidemiology numbers are cited from prior meta-analyses, not generated here.

## Claim-ready facts
- **Design:** narrative review of DR treatment and ocular drug-delivery systems.
- **Population / model:** people with type 1 and type 2 diabetes and DR/DME as discussed clinically; nanocarrier examples are largely diabetic rats/mice, OIR models, and zebrafish as quoted from cited papers.
- **n:** not reported (review).
- **Intervention / comparator:** laser photocoagulation, vitrectomy, repeated intravitreal anti-VEGF and steroids versus novel receptor ligands, gene therapy, and nanocarrier platforms (liposomes, PLGA NPs, SLN/NLC, dendrimers, peptide eye drops).
- **Primary endpoint:** not applicable as original data; organising outcomes are vision preservation, injection frequency, posterior-segment bioavailability, and whether a carrier can cross the blood–retinal barrier (BRB).
- **Primary result:** Current DR treatments (anti-VEGF, steroids, laser, vitrectomy) have limitations and adverse effects; topical delivery is “nearly ineffective” for posterior-segment DR because bioavailability is usually <5%, while systemic delivery yields only 1% to 2% of drug in the vitreous, so intraocular injection is used despite invasiveness; nanocarriers are synthesised as a way to improve permeability, retention, sustained release, and RPE targeting (Abstract; Section 4).
- **Cannot show:** comparative effectiveness of named nanocarriers in patients with DR; original trial outcomes; a validated human toxicity model for nanoparticles.
- **Epidemiology the review quotes:** DR “affects almost 103.12 million people worldwide”; a 2020 meta-analysis is cited as placing DR among leading causes of irreversible blindness over age 50; between 1990 and 2015, people blinded due to DR increased from 2 million to 4 million and those with visual impairment from 1.4 million to 2.6 million (Introduction).
- **Mechanism (cited / proposed, not measured here):** DR as a neurovascular-unit disease (neurons, glia, vessels) with NPDR progressing via pericyte loss, VEGF-driven neovascularization to PDR; ROS, AGEs, PKC, polyol and hexosamine pathways; HbA1c “accounts for only 6.6% in the variation of the DR risk” (Section 2, cited). Anti-VEGF does not completely inhibit DR progression; frequent injections increase endophthalmitis risk (Introduction, cited).
- **Barrier numbers stated by the review:** topical bioavailability usually <5%; systemic administration ~1% to 2% reaching the vitreous (Section 4).

## Key findings
- Anti-VEGF agents tabulated (Table 1) with molecular weights the review lists: bevacizumab 148 kDa; ranibizumab 48 kDa; aflibercept 115 kDa; faricimab 150 kDa; brolucizumab 26 kDa; conbercept 143 kDa. (Aflibercept 115 kDa here differs from other reviews’ ~145 kDa — see Open questions.)
- Review quotes that high-dose ranibizumab (2.0 vs 0.5 mg) in a DME population showed no statistically significant difference in letters gained through months 6 and 24 (Section 3.3.1, citing the DME study population).
- Faricimab is described as the first bispecific antibody against Ang-2 and VEGF-A; the review quotes phase 3 data as adjustable dosing extending up to every 16 weeks versus aflibercept in DME, and every-8-week treatment benefits on vision and CST (Section 3.3.1, cited). Mechanism of dual Ang-2/VEGF-A blockade is cited, not measured here.
- Review quotes that intravitreal anti-VEGF “increases the risk of dialysis, by up to 85% compared with untreated patients” (Section 3.3.1, cited) — renal adverse-effect claim is from cited literature, not a new analysis.
- Steroids: glucocorticoids are strongly anti-inflammatory and can down-regulate VEGF-A, but systemic use raises blood glucose and topical/intravitreal use is limited by cataract and glucocorticoid-induced glaucoma (Section 3.3.2). Triamcinolone acetonide is called “currently the most effective steroid drug for the treatment of PDR”; dendrimer-conjugated TA is cited as more anti-inflammatory than free TA with fewer side effects (cited).
- Intraocular routes for DR: intravitreal, subretinal, and suprachoroidal injections bypass anterior barriers but carry haemorrhage, IOP rise, retinal detachment, cataract, and endophthalmitis; ILM limits outer-retina/RPE concentrations after vitreous injection, driving repeat dosing (Section 4). Subretinal injection contacts photoreceptors/RPE but is technically difficult. Periocular diffusion through sclera is passive and limited. Suprachoroidal modes listed: catheter, hypodermic needle, surgical implant, microinjector.
- Nanocarriers: PLGA NPs are described as FDA-approved for clinical use because of biocompatibility and non-toxic degradation products (Section 4.2, citing Tahara et al.). Examples quoted: pioglitazone-loaded surface-modified PLGA NPs after topical administration in diabetic rats lowered VEGF; chitosan NP / PLGA-PEG-PLGA hydrogel insulin via subconjunctival injection was neuroprotective in DR rats; HA-encapsulated BSA NPs of apatinib as a topical VEGFR2 inhibitor; chitosan-modified 5-FU NLCs in a rat DR model.
- Review quotes a nanopeptide (SSP) eye-drop study as achieving the same effect as invasive antibody injections, and that SSP plus anti-VEGF “can increase the current clinical therapeutic effect by 50%” (Section 4.3, citing reference 154). Attribute as review-quoted, not independently verified here.
- Translational limits named by the authors: nanoparticle complexity (composition, charge, ligands) makes toxicity hard to analyse; no suitable models for nano-immune interactions; poor reproducibility/scale-up; cost; lack of quality standards; incomplete understanding of in vivo fate by route (end of Section 4).

## Limitations (as stated by the authors, or evident from the methods)
- Pathogenesis of DR “has not been fully elucidated” (Introduction).
- Gene-therapy vectors: relatively low transfection, potential genotoxicity, need for long-term monitoring (Section 3, gene therapy).
- Nanomedicine translation limited by biological complexity, individual heterogeneity, and non-reproducible synthesis (Section 4).
- Narrative review; many nanocarrier efficacy claims are single cited animal studies.
- Text extraction has PDF line-break artefacts (DEM for DME in a figure caption).

## Relevance to our research question
DR-specific map of why anti-VEGF and steroids must be injected, what each ocular route actually overcomes or fails to overcome, and what nanocarriers have been tested in DR models — plus explicit translation/toxicity gaps.
