# Ocular Drug Delivery: a Comprehensive Review

- **Citation (as given in the paper / filename):** Ahmed S, Amin MM, Sayed S. AAPS PharmSciTech. 2023;24:66. doi:10.1208/s12249-023-02516-9
- **Source file:** papers/2026-09-20-dr-drug-delivery-found/Ahmed2023_25169.txt
- **Extracted:** 2026-09-20
- **Screening:** Included (comprehensive ocular-delivery review that names DR and DME among posterior diseases and routes)

## Research question
How do ocular anatomy, barriers, administration routes, dosage forms, and nanocarriers determine whether a drug reaches its ophthalmic target, and which strategies improve residence time, permeation, and bioavailability?

## Methods
Narrative review (Review Article). Walks through eye anatomy, major ocular diseases (including DR), precorneal/corneal/blood-ocular barriers, routes (topical, intracameral, intravitreal/implants, juxtascleral, retrobulbar, subconjunctival, and others), dosage forms, nanostructured platforms, characterisation methods, and strategies to enhance bioavailability (Abstract; Conclusions). No systematic search protocol or included-study count.

## Sample / data
Secondary pharmaceutics literature and tabulated examples of routes and nanocarriers (Table I and later tables). Disease burden numbers are cited from prior work. Not a DR-only sample.

## Claim-ready facts
- **Design:** narrative review of ocular drug delivery (anatomy, barriers, routes, dosage forms, nanocarriers).
- **Population / model:** human ocular anatomy and diseases (cataract, glaucoma, AMD, DR, retinoblastoma, fungal keratitis); cited in vitro, ex vivo, and in vivo characterisation of nanocarriers.
- **n:** not reported (review).
- **Intervention / comparator:** conventional topical liquids versus other routes (intravitreal, periocular, devices) and nanocarrier systems intended to prolong residence and permeation.
- **Primary endpoint:** not applicable as original data; organising metrics are ocular bioavailability, residence time, and whether a route can serve posterior disease.
- **Primary result:** Distinctive ocular anatomy hinders drugs from reaching target sites; conventional topical drops are wasted by blinking and tear flow so bioavailability is “minimized to less than 5%,” and “more than 95% of marketed products exists in liquid state” / topical administration “represents more than 95% of marketed ocular products”; nanocarriers are attractive because they can entrap hydrophilic and lipophilic drugs, enhance permeability, sustain residence, improve stability, and augment bioavailability — yet effective management “remains a difficult mission” because of anterior and posterior obstacles (Abstract; Introduction; Routes; Conclusions).
- **Cannot show:** original DR/DME visual outcomes; which nanocarrier is best for anti-VEGF vs corticosteroid retinal delivery; human PK of the tabulated examples as a class.
- **DR-specific statements in this paper:** DR is a vascular complication of both diabetes types; “About 60% of patients of type II and all patients of type I diabetes have a certain extent of retinopathy after 20 years of diabetes”; third major trigger to blindness in the USA after cataract and corneal blindness; managed with laser, vitrectomy, and pharmacological treatments. Pharmacological options named: intravitreous corticosteroids to decrease macular swelling; “sustained release corticosteroids implant that interferes the inflammatory pathways”; modern anti-VEGF (ranibizumab and aflibercept) to reduce leakage and edema (section Diabetic Retinopathy).
- **Barrier / anatomy numbers stated:** cul-de-sac maximum capacity 30 μL in humans, reduced to 70–80% when the lower eyelid returns (and further reduced by inflammation); cornea epithelium barrier to hydrophilic/large molecules, stroma barrier to lipophilic drugs; BAB anterior, BRB posterior (Obstacles; Anatomy).
- **Mechanism (cited / proposed):** tear turnover, nasolacrimal drainage, and corneal sandwich (lipophilic epithelium / hydrophilic stroma / endothelium) limit topical absorption; BRB and BAB limit systemic and topical access to the posterior segment (cited). Nanocarriers proposed to shield drug, sustain residence, and improve corneal permeation (cited as a class, not newly measured).

## Key findings
- Disease burden quoted: approximately 76 million people with glaucoma, 196 million with AMD, and 92.6 million with DR; cataract causes about 40–60% of blindness worldwide (Introduction).
- Intravitreal injections/implants deliver medicine into the vitreous “close to the retina.” Examples quoted: vitamin E/PLGA microspheres with GDNF prolonged release for 6 months; polymer-free dexamethasone dimer implants similar; “intravitreal injection of the biodegradable Rho kinase and protein kinase C inhibitor for handling diabetic macular edema and neovascular age-related macular degeneration exhibited prolonged release for about 6 months” (Intravitreal Injections/Implants).
- Juxtascleral injections are used for posterior conditions “that cannot be handled through conventional topical route,” including “diabetic-related conditions”; anecortave cortisone for AMD quoted as prolonged release for 6 months in choroid and retina; trans-scleral microneedles for AAV retinal gene treatment are named.
- Retrobulbar triamcinolone is mentioned for macular edema from retinal vein occlusion (not DR-specific).
- Subconjunctival injection is used when topical penetration into the anterior segment is very low.
- Topical route is “unsuitable for handicapped and elder patients” and needs frequent high-dose concentration with possible systemic side effects via nasolacrimal absorption (Topical Administration).
- Vitrectomy for DR “provides only short-term relief and does not obstruct further leaking of blood”; laser closes leaky vessels but results in laser scar (DR section).
- Conclusions: approaches aim to minimise dosing interval, administered dose, and unwanted effects and to enhance retention, permeation, and bioavailability via controlled/sustained systems; authors predict further innovations for compliance and eye health.

## Limitations (as stated by the authors, or evident from the methods)
- Comprehensive scope across all ocular disease, so DR/DME delivery is a minority of the text.
- Many tabulated nanocarrier examples are anterior-segment (antifungal, glaucoma, dry eye) rather than retinal anti-VEGF.
- No search protocol or study count; characterisation methods are described at the platform level.
- Clinical-trial examples in “Innovations in Clinical Trials” (pilocarpine cream, Cequa, Dextenza) are not DR implants.

## Relevance to our research question
Supplies the barrier and route framework (why topical <5% never treats the diabetic retina; why intravitreal/implants and periocular injections exist) and names corticosteroid implants and anti-VEGF as current DR pharmacology. Less specific on named commercial DME implants than Kim/Tsung.
