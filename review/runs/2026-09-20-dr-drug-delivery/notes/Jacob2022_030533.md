# Lipid Nanoparticles as a Promising Drug Delivery Carrier for Topical Ocular Therapy—An Overview on Recent Advances

- **Citation (as given in the paper / filename):** Jacob S, Nair AB, Shah J, Gupta S, Boddu SHS, Sreeharsha N, Joseph A, Shinu P, Morsy MA. Pharmaceutics. 2022;14(3):533. doi:10.3390/pharmaceutics14030533
- **Source file:** papers/2026-09-20-dr-drug-delivery-found/Jacob2022_030533.txt
- **Extracted:** 2026-09-20
- **Screening:** Included (narrative formulation review of SLN, NLC, nanoemulsion, and liposomes for topical/ocular delivery, including posterior-segment and selected DR-related examples)

## Research question
How have solid lipid nanoparticles, nanostructured lipid carriers, nanoemulsions, and liposomes been used to overcome ocular barriers for topical (and related) therapy, and what formulation parameters, characterization methods, and clinical-trial examples support their use in anterior and posterior disease?

## Methods
Narrative review of lipid nanocarriers for ocular delivery. Sections cover ocular anatomy and barriers, transport mechanisms, SLN components and manufacturing (high-pressure homogenization, solvent methods, etc.), in vitro characterization, mucoadhesive/cationic functionalization, NLC and nanoemulsion case tables, liposomes, and tabulated clinical trials (Table 5). No new experiment.

## Sample / data
Secondary literature. Worked examples in Tables 3–5 include named drugs (natamycin, triamcinolone acetonide, bimatoprost, valacyclovir, tobramycin, curcumin, dexamethasone, flurbiprofen, itraconazole, clarithromycin) with particle sizes, zeta, EE%, and selected PK numbers as reported by the cited primary studies.

## Claim-ready facts
- **Design:** narrative review (not original research)
- **Population / model:** not applicable (synthesis of published animal, ex vivo, and a few clinical lipid-nanocarrier studies)
- **n:** not applicable for the review itself; cited clinical-trial enrollments include TLC399/ProDex n=31 (NCT03093701) and others in Table 5
- **Intervention / comparator:** SLN, NLC, nanoemulsion, and liposome formulations vs conventional drops, suspensions, or free drug, as reported in the source papers
- **Primary endpoint:** not applicable (no single review-level primary endpoint)
- **Primary result:** Authors state topical solution ocular bioavailability is only 5–10%, with blinking 6–15 times/min, tear turnover 0.5–2.2 µL/min, and conjunctival-sac retention volume ~30 µL (Introduction; Barriers). SLN size is described as 10–1000 nm; pores of ocular tissues are said to assist intracellular transport of 200–300 nm SLN/NLC, while ~100 nm particles are engulfed by receptor-mediated phagocytosis (Posterior-segment paragraph). Cited SLN examples: natamycin SLN 42 nm, zeta 26 mV, EE ~85%, 10 h release; triamcinolone SLN/gel trans-corneal permeability 10.2- and 9.3-fold vs suspension; valacyclovir SLN AUC0–12 856.47 ± 7.86 vs solution 470.75 ± 8.91 µg·h/mL; tobramycin-SLN “deeper penetration to the retina.” Cited NLC: curcumin 66.8 ± 2 nm, EE 96 ± 1.6%; dexamethasone NLC-gel AUC0–12h one-fold vs NLC and three-fold vs tobramycin-dexamethasone drops; flurbiprofen NLC 228.3 nm, PDI 0.156, zeta −33.3 mV, EE ~90%. Chitosan-coated triamcinolone liposomes: loading 74%, zeta +41.1 mV; 8 h release 93% uncoated vs 83% (0.2% chitosan) and 73% (0.3% chitosan); drug detected in ocular tissues after 2 weeks in a CNV rat model. PEG-coated cationic liposomes absorbed to retina <1 h and remained 24 h ex vivo; calcein vitreous half-life 45-fold vs solution. DR-specific original experiment in this paper: none. Cited DR-related: SLNs containing siRNA against HuR reduced HuR and VEGF vs naked siRNA (no n/% in this review’s prose). TLC399 (ProDex) Phase 2 is macular edema from retinal vein occlusion, not DME (NCT03093701, n=31).
- **Cannot show:** a pooled effect of lipid NPs in human DR/DME; this review did not generate new EE%, size, or in vivo retinal data

## Key findings
- Static (cornea, blood–aqueous and blood–retinal barriers), dynamic (tear turnover, conjunctival/choroidal flow), and metabolic barriers limit posterior delivery after drops (Sections 3–4).
- SLN limitations listed: particle growth, aggregation, polymorphic transition, low loading of hydrophilic drugs, and burst from surface-adsorbed drug (5.1).
- NLC vs SLN: authors report NLCs more stable at room temperature; SLN release slower than NLC at low loading, similar at high loading (5.2).
- Dexamethasone-NLC hybrid hydrogel: 88.65% cumulative release to 72 h vs dexamethasone-NLC 93.10% within 48 h (cited in vitro).
- Authors’ own prior clarithromycin SLN (cited): ~80% release in 8 h; goat cornea flux 30.45 vs 10.94 µg/cm²/h (p<0.0001); rabbit Cmax ~1066 ng/mL, 150% increase and 2.8-fold AUC vs control (5.1.4).
- **Mechanism grade:** mucoadhesion, tight-junction opening by surfactants, and phagocytosis of ~100 nm particles are **cited** from prior work, not newly measured.

## Limitations (as stated by the authors, or evident from the methods)
- Review snapshot (received Jan 2022); not systematic; no PRISMA counts.
- Most tabulated examples are anterior-segment anti-infectives, glaucoma, or inflammation; posterior DR/DME clinical evidence is thin.
- TLC399 trial listed under macular edema is RVO, not diabetic macular edema.
- HuR-siRNA SLN DR claim has no particle size, n, or p-value in this text.
- Authors note SLN/NLC thermodynamic instability and drug expulsion on lipid crystallization.

## Relevance to our research question
Maps lipid nanocarriers as topical/periocular tools that can, in cited studies, raise corneal flux, extend precorneal residence, and in isolated examples reach retina or carry corticosteroids/anti-VEGF-path cargo. It does not itself prove anti-VEGF or steroid delivery in DR/DME.
