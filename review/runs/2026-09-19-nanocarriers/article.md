# Nanocarriers that arrive still have to work: a narrative review of release triggers, stealth coatings, and mucosal constraints

## Abstract

A drug that is potent in a dish often fails in a body because it never reaches the tissue at a useful concentration, or because it gets there and stays locked inside a particle. Liposomes and related lipid vesicles were invented to change that geography: they hide a payload in a water core or in a bilayer without rewriting the molecule. Circulation-friendly coatings, especially polyethylene glycol (PEG), help vesicles last long enough to accumulate in leaky tumours, yet the same coating can keep a pH-sensitive membrane from opening in an acidifying endosome. Stimulus-responsive designs try to empty the vesicle with local acid, enzymes, redox chemistry, heat, light, or ultrasound. Infection adds a different trap. Bacteria in biofilms sit behind polysaccharide and DNA, so a planktonic minimum inhibitory concentration understates the dose at the site. Silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) can restore or sharpen antibiotic activity in vitro, including against vancomycin-resistant enterococci and carbapenem-resistant Pseudomonas, but they are not liposomes and a human dose window is not established here. The nose is a third geography: even a simple commercial spray lands differently when actuation angle, airflow, and temperature change. This review follows those three problems in order: how membranes are built to release, how polymer coats and charge change the object, and how metal–antibiotic combinations and inlet physics sit beside vesicle work rather than inside it. A PEGylated thermosensitive doxorubicin liposome that reached phase III did not meet its primary progression-free survival endpoint in hepatocellular carcinoma when paired with ablation. That clinical miss, and the laboratory PEG-versus-acid results, are not a single slogan about stealth. They are different membranes answering different cues. The practical implication is to name the cue, the coat, and the route in the same experiment, and not to treat a checkerboard synergy index as a nasal or tumour product.

liposomes; pH-sensitive membranes; PEGylation; pectin coating; silver nanoparticles; nasal deposition; biofilm; thermosensitive doxorubicin

## 1. Introduction

A small-molecule drug is asked to do two incompatible things. It must be soluble and stable enough to be given, and it must still arrive at a tumour, an infected mucosa, or an intracellular compartment at a concentration that matches its target. Many agents fail the second job while remaining chemically intact. They are cleared by the kidney or liver, bind the wrong proteins, never leave the blood, or never leave the particle that was meant to carry them.

Liposomes exist because a phospholipid bilayer in water closes on itself. The closed shell, a vesicle, can trap water-soluble drug in the aqueous interior and lipophilic drug in the membrane. The covalent structure of the active molecule does not have to change. What changes is distribution: a payload that would have been diluted, hydrolysed, or taken up by the wrong cells can be delayed, concentrated, or redirected. That is why liposomal doxorubicin and several lipid anti-infective or vaccine products already exist in practice [1], [2]. It is also why a new vesicle recipe is not automatically a new therapy. Packaging is a pharmacokinetic intervention. It still has to empty.

Tumours and infected tissues are not well-stirred tanks. Solid tumours often have irregular endothelium and poor lymphatic drainage. Macromolecules and nanoparticles can therefore accumulate by the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect: they leak out of defective vessels and leave slowly [3], [4]. The same physics is less reliable in humans than in many mouse models, and it does not by itself decide intracellular fate. A vesicle that has extravasated can sit in the interstitium. A vesicle that has been endocytosed can sit in an endosome. In both places the drug is geographically “at the tumour” and pharmacologically unused.

Endosomes acidify as they mature. Early endosomal pH is often near 6.5; late endosomes and lysosomes are commonly discussed around 5–5.5 in liposome papers [5], [6], [7]. Many tumours also have a mildly acidic extracellular space because of glycolytic metabolism, sometimes near 6.5–6.8 and always heterogeneous. Those two acidities are not the same compartment. A formulation tuned to dump a fluorescent dye at pH 5.5 in a cuvette is usually being asked to mimic endosomal escape, not extracellular tumour acid, unless the authors say otherwise. Heat, light, ultrasound, and magnetic fields are different cues again: they are applied from outside the body, so the geography of release is the geography of the applicator [3], [6]. Enzymes and glutathione-rich cytosol are chemical scissors that appear after the vesicle has already been taken up or has reached a reducing interior [6], [8].

The usual answer to short blood half-life is a stealth coating. PEG is a flexible, well-hydrated polymer. When a small mole fraction of PEG-lipid is mixed into a vesicle, the chains extend into water and reduce protein adsorption and macrophage uptake. The vesicle lasts longer and has more time to use leaky vasculature. The cost is a particle that may be too inert to fuse, too well hidden to be taken up, or too sterically crowded to undergo the membrane rearrangement that an acid trigger needs [5], [7], [9]. Short PEG and long PEG are not equivalent steric objects. A few mole percent of PEG2000 is a different surface from PEG750, and both are different from an uncoated bilayer.

The lipid physics behind pH-sensitive liposomes is a shape argument. A bilayer made largely of phosphatidylcholine is relatively cylindrical and prefers a flat lamellar phase, the phase that can enclose water. Dioleoylphosphatidylethanolamine (DOPE) is more cone-shaped. Alone, it prefers an inverted hexagonal phase that does not hold an aqueous core. Mixing DOPE with a titratable lipid such as cholesteryl hemisuccinate (CHEMS) keeps the membrane lamellar at plasma pH, when the succinate is ionized and hydrated, and allows hexagonal-leaning disruption when the headgroup is protonated [5], [7]. That is one family of “smart” liposomes. Heat-sensitive designs use another trick: a bilayer whose gel-to-fluid transition sits a few degrees above 37 °C, sometimes with lysolipids that stabilize pores at the transition [2]. Dual-responsive recipes try to require more than one cue [8]. None of these families is a lipid nanoparticle of the nucleic-acid type. Those particles are typically ionizable-lipid cores without the same interior water space; stimuli language does not transfer unchanged from a DOPE/CHEMS liposome to an mRNA product [4].

Infection adds a matrix that tumours do not always have. Bacteria in a biofilm secrete polysaccharides, proteins, and DNA. Diffusion slows, metabolism changes, and the biofilm phenotype can raise antibiotic tolerance by as much as about a thousandfold relative to planktonic cultures [10]. A minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) measured on free-swimming cells therefore understates the dose needed at the site. Two pharmaceutical answers appear in this literature, and they are not the same object. One is to put the antibiotic in a lipid vesicle so that it is protected and, in some designs, can fuse with bacterial membranes [1]. The other is to combine a conventional antibiotic with silver nanoparticles (AgNPs), which damage membranes and DNA with little target specificity [11], [12], [13], [14]. Synergy in a checkerboard assay is a bet on restored susceptibility, not on a single receptor. Colistin, vancomycin, and aminoglycosides are chemically unlike one another; a fractional inhibitory concentration (FIC) that is synergistic for one pair does not transfer to another without measurement.

Mucosal delivery, especially the nose, is a third geography. The respiratory epithelium of the turbinates is the usual target for local uptake. An in vitro inlet that copies average adult geometry can rank sprays, but only if the experiment admits that angle, sniff, and temperature interact [15]. Nasal air is heated and humidified in life. A droplet’s viscosity, evaporation, and bounce depend on temperature. A nanocarrier that is perfect in a vial can still miss the turbinate if the plume hits the vestibule. Polymer coats such as pectin are a separate mucosal tactic: they change size and charge, often toward a larger, anionic object intended for the gut or mucus, rather than adding a chemical trigger [16], [17]. Charge itself is not a therapeutic class. Cationic adjuvant vesicles, anionic sertraline vesicles, and negatively charged AgNPs are different electrostatic stories [16], [11], [18].

The poorly mapped region is therefore not “are nanocarriers interesting.” It is which knob a paper turned, and whether that knob answers the failure mode it claims to answer. PEG can be a circulation necessity and a pH-trigger tax at the same time, because lyso-thermosensitive doxorubicin membranes and DOPE/CHEMS membranes are not the same bilayer [5], [9], [2]. AgNP–antibiotic synergy can be strong in vitro and still say nothing about a liposomal nasal spray. An idealized inlet can rank a commercial mist without ever seeing a vesicle.

The aim of this review is to state what the included studies measured about release triggers, stealth coatings, polymer coats, metal–antibiotic combinations, and inlet deposition, and to keep those measurements in their own designs. The central argument is that accumulation is not efficacy, and that PEG length, pectin coats, checkerboard FIC values, and actuation angle are design facts rather than a single clinical platform.

## 2. Methods

This is a narrative review of retrieved full texts, not a meta-analysis and not a pooled effect size. Starting papers covered stimuli-responsive liposomes, pectin-coated liposomes, pH-sensitive PEGylated membranes, sertraline-loaded anionic liposomes, AgNP–colistin and AgNP–vancomycin combinations, nasal-inlet spray deposition, and a polymersome approach to biofilm antibiotics. Related records were taken from OpenAlex as open-access journal articles from 2021 through 2026, ranked by relevance, then title-screened for liposomes, polymersomes, AgNPs, pectin, or nasal inlets. Inclusion required a readable full text. Older starting papers were kept when they defined the stimulus map. After the first comparison table, extra search targeted clinical thermosensitive doxorubicin and nanocarrier treatment of lung biofilms.

One polymersome report on acid-triggered tobramycin release with a doripenem partner vesicle was not available as a complete article and is not used as a results source. Where a 2020 journal of formulation science file was not public, an open 2020 paper on anionic sertraline liposomes in the *Asian Journal of Pharmaceutics* was used and is labelled as such. ## 3. Why a trigger is needed

Long-circulating vesicles were already a success story when stimulus-responsive liposomes were proposed. The remaining failures, as stated in the 2017 cancer-liposome map, were inefficient release at the target and endosomal trapping [3]. External cues in that map are hyperthermia, magnetic fields, light, and ultrasound; internal cues are pH, reduction, and enzymes [3]. A methods review covering November 2008 to February 2016 makes the same mechanical claim in different words: triggered release is generally a local bilayer defect and destabilization, not a new covalent prodrug [6]. Those two papers are secondary literature. They do not contribute a new n. They do fix the problem statement that later original papers inherit.

By 2025 the endogenous list had lengthened to include reactive oxygen species and hypoxia, and dual- or multi-responsive vesicles were offered as a way to raise precision and reduce systemic leak [8]. Dual-responsive designs can, in principle, require two cues before the membrane fails. This sample contains no original dual-responsive experiment, so that claim stays at review level [8]. A colorectal-focused review applies the catalogue to 5-fluorouracil, a hydrophilic, short-lived backbone of colorectal regimens [19]. Hydrophilic cargo is the interior-core use case for liposomes, and it is the use case that most needs a trigger, because a stable bilayer will otherwise hold the drug through the wrong tissues. No 5-fluorouracil mouse number from that review is treated as a new finding here [19].

A 2025 lipid-nanocarrier review puts liposomes beside lipid nanoparticles for nucleic acids and states that clinical translation of active targeting remains extremely hard even when the chemistry looks specific [4]. Ligand decoration and stimulus-responsive release are often advertised together. They solve different steps. A ligand may change which cell takes the particle up. A trigger changes whether the payload leaves. The shared lesson from that review is only that formulation papers have outrun clinical targeting success [4].

Taken together, the maps in Table 2 justify reading PEG papers, coating papers, and clinical thermosensitive doxorubicin as tests of a known trade-off, not as isolated recipes. They cannot replace a measurement of leak, uptake, tumour volume, MIC, or regional deposition.

## 4. Polyethylene glycol versus pH-sensitive membranes

If the cue is endosomal acid and the membrane is DOPE/CHEMS, PEG is not a decorative stealth layer. It is a structural perturbation of the same bilayer that is supposed to fail.

Calcein-loaded DOPE–cholesteryl hemisuccinate vesicles have been reported to release 55% of dye at pH 5.5 when they are not PEGylated, 24% with 0.6 mol% DSPE-PEG2000, and 10% with 3 mol% [5]. Those percentages are experimental literature cited inside a molecular-dynamics paper, not new wet-lab curves from that paper. The simulations ask why the polymer hurts the trigger. Anionized cholesteryl hemisuccinate hydrates the interface and supports a lamellar DOPE membrane at pH 7.4. Protonation reduces hydrophilicity and area per lipid, a change that, on molecular-shape arguments, favours leaving the lamellar state. PEG chains penetrate the DOPE bilayer, raise area per lipid, and hydrogen-bond in ways that phosphatidylcholine membranes are said not to support as readily, because phosphatidylcholine lacks hydrogen-bond donors [5]. Area per lipid is not a clinical endpoint. It is a reason that hexagonal transition can be delayed.

Cell experiments with the same lipid idea show that PEG length is not a binary switch. DOPE:CHEMS vesicles below 200 nm held calcein at pH 7.4 in every formulation tested, which is the safety half of the endosomal clock [7]. Insertion of PEG2000 cut acid-triggered leak relative to PEG750 or unPEGylated pH-responsive vesicles, which matches the lamellar-stabilization story [7]. Uptake of calcein in GL261 glioblastoma cells fell when PEG2000 was present; differences between PEG lengths were described as negligible for uptake in the paper’s abstract [7]. Entrapment of calcein was low (about 0.2–0.5%), so the study is a mechanistic dye study, not a glioblastoma drug product [7]. Calcein is used because self-quenching reports release. It is not doxorubicin.

A doxorubicin version of pH-sensitive vesicles was taken into 4T1 tumour-bearing mice. Non-PEGylated and PEGylated particles were similar in size (on the order of 140 nm), polydispersity below 0.2, and encapsulation above 90% [9]. Cryo-electron microscopy showed similar morphology, so efficacy differences are more likely to come from surface PEG and uptake than from a size-bin change [9]. Cellular uptake favoured the non-PEGylated doxorubicin liposomes. In mice (n=7 per group), those non-PEGylated vesicles inhibited tumour growth by 60.4% and left no histologic lung metastases in that arm, which the authors read as a reason not to add PEG by default [9]. Lip-DOX was reported at 139.4 ± 3.8 nm with 93.1 ± 1.2% encapsulation [9]. That in vivo result does not contradict the need for PEG in long-circulating commercial products. It says that in this pH-sensitive doxorubicin design, stealth cost uptake and efficacy in 4T1.

Primary experimental endpoints for these and the other original studies are summarised in Table 1. The PEG–acid chain of inference is mechanistic, not regulatory: simulation of area per lipid, dye leak in buffer and in GL261 cells, then a mouse efficacy arm with a real drug. No paper in that chain is a human trial of pH-sensitive DOPE liposomes.

A vesicle taken up by endocytosis has minutes to hours before lysosomal hydrolases degrade a peptide cargo. pH-sensitive DOPE membranes are an attempt to use that clock. If PEG delays hexagonal transition until after the vesicle is destroyed, the design fails even if plasma half-life looks excellent. Lack of leak at pH 7.4 is necessary; reduced leak at acid pH with PEG2000 is the failure mode [7].

## 5. Thermosensitive doxorubicin in the clinic

Heat-triggered vesicles that use lysolipids and a sharp melting transition are a different membrane from DOPE/CHEMS. Lyso-thermosensitive liposomal doxorubicin (LTLD; DPPC:MSPC:DSPE-PEG2000 86:10:4) was built so that locoregional hyperthermia or radiofrequency ablation would open pores near the gel-to-fluid transition while PEG still supported circulation [2]. A 2023 review of liposomal doxorubicin records that LTLD, given at 50 mg/m2 fifteen minutes before radiofrequency ablation, failed the primary progression-free survival endpoint of the HEAT phase III trial versus ablation alone in 3–7 cm hepatocellular carcinoma; overall survival was similar in the main analysis [2]. A subgroup with ablation lasting at least 45 minutes had better overall survival [2]. Phase I work in 24 patients had shown a dose–response on time to treatment failure and allowed a jump to phase III [2].

The clinical message is therefore not that thermosensitive liposomes cannot be made. It is that a PEGylated heat-triggered doxorubicin product plus ablation did not win its pre-specified progression-free survival comparison. Ablation that lasts 45 minutes is not the same energy deposition as a shorter burn. Future thermosensitive trials that ignore time-at-temperature will mix drug-release physics with surgical technique. Time to treatment failure in 24 patients is also a different endpoint from phase III progression-free survival. Changing endpoint and widening tumour-size eligibility at the same time makes a missed primary result hard to invert in a narrative [2].

Ferreira’s account of lipid anti-infectives and vaccines shows that liposomes can be products [1]. HEAT shows that a sophisticated thermosensitive doxorubicin liposome can still miss a primary endpoint [2]. Manufacturing success is not the same as a positive phase III on the endpoint that was written down. PEG in LTLD is part of a heat-pore recipe. PEG in DOPE/CHEMS is part of an acid-hexagonal recipe. A review that concluded “never PEG” or “always PEG” would be ignoring Table 1 and Table 2 at once.

## 6. Coats, charge, and gastrointestinal aims

Pectin is an anionic polysaccharide used as a coat, not as a bilayer lipid. Fragile phospholipid membranes motivate coatings because hydrolysis, oxidation, and gastrointestinal insult are separate threats from blood clearance [17]. Chitosan, pectin, and alginate appear repeatedly as answers to those threats, in a literature that mixes food and pharmaceutical examples [17]. A coat that survives a shelf can still peel in bile. Storage stability is not in vivo stability.

Cationic CAF04 liposomes (DDA:MMG) were coated in a microfluidic chip with low-methoxyl, amidated, or high-methoxyl pectin [16]. Batch coating is sensitive to mixing order and local concentration. A chip with defined flow-rate ratios converts those accidents into parameters. A pectin/liposome weight ratio of 0.7 and a 2:1 pectin:liposome flow-rate ratio were selected as optimal [16]. Uncoated particles remained near 220 nm through the chip; coated particles were at least threefold larger, with strongly negative zeta potentials for low-methoxyl types (−38 to −40 mV) and a less negative value for high-methoxyl pectin (−18 ± 3.68 mV) [16]. Low-methoxyl pectin liposomes were 965 ± 178 nm (n=3 for size and zeta) [16]. Microscopy was interpreted as a pectin network with liposomes inside or attached, and the coat was reported as stable on storage [16]. Polydispersity remaining moderate while diameter jumps from 220 nm to about 1 µm means the object in suspension is no longer a small unilamellar vesicle even if liposomes still exist inside a pectin mesh [16]. Downstream filtration, spraying, or injection would see a different aerodynamic or pharmacokinetic object than uncoated CAF04. The paper does not measure intestinal survival or mucin binding. It measures process control.

Anionic sertraline hydrochloride liposomes made by film hydration are a charge example outside infection and cancer. Mean size was 154.10 nm and entrapment 69.46% ± 1, with Higuchi kinetics [18]. Rats received 5 mg/kg in a chronic unpredictable mild stress protocol; the authors report behavioural differences versus a marketed tablet [18]. Group n is not reported as a number in the extracted methods. The direction of the immobility readout should be read against the paper’s scoring rules before anyone treats it as a definitive antidepressant assay [18]. Human pharmacokinetics from a related 2020 journal article were not in the public file used here.

CAF04 starts cationic so that pectin can adsorb [16]. Sertraline liposomes are made anionic [18]. Zeta potential is not a therapeutic class. Comparing Lutta’s micrometre-scale pectin objects with Nunes’s ~140 nm doxorubicin vesicles as if they were the same pharmaceutical species would confuse a polymer mesh with a unilamellar vesicle [16], [9].

Taken together, polymer coats are a standard answer to fragile phospholipid membranes, and microfluidics can impose a reproducible pectin coat on a cationic adjuvant vesicle. This heading cannot show that pectin-coated CAF04 improves a clinical endpoint, protects drug in intestinal fluid, or deposits in a nose.

## 7. Silver–antibiotic combinations

AgNPs are not liposomes. They appear in this review because the starting papers asked whether metal particles can restore antibiotic activity where vesicles are also being asked to treat infection, including biofilms. The honest synthesis is parallel tactics, not a hybrid product, unless a paper actually conjugates or encapsulates both.

Checkerboard assays dilute two agents together. For each, the MIC in combination is divided by the MIC alone; the sum is the FIC index. Values at or below 0.5 are commonly called synergy, values above 4 antagonism, and the middle band additive or indifferent depending on the paper’s cut-offs [13], [14]. Reporting FIC, MIC fold-drop, and percent loading as one “AgNP synergy effect size” would hide those definitions. Table 3 collects the numbers that can be compared only loosely.

Colistin conjugated to AgNPs (optimized formulation F3) had 11.55 ± 0.93% drug loading and a strongly negative zeta potential [11]. Against *E. coli*, *Klebsiella pneumoniae*, and *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* indicator strains, Col-AgNP MIC/MBC values sat in a 4–16 µg/mL band, with F3 highlighted at 4 µg/mL, while unconjugated AgNPs were much less potent (64 µg/mL bacteriostatic and 128 µg/mL bactericidal in the comparison given) [11]. Free colistin remained more potent on MIC (1 µg/mL) [11]. Compatibility with red cells and renal cells held to 16 µg/mL; at 32 µg/mL the conjugate showed higher cell survival than AgNPs or colistin in the authors’ cytotoxicity readout, with 11.61 ± 0.84% hemolysis for Col-AgNPs versus a higher value for AgNPs [11]. Mechanism claims include membrane disruption and DNA damage [11]. The comparison that the table supports is therefore conjugate versus AgNPs plus a possible toxicity offset, not a claim that conjugation beats colistin on MIC. TISTR collection strains are reproducible tools. They are not a burn-unit antibiogram.

Ten clinical carbapenem-resistant *P. aeruginosa* isolates, all strong biofilm formers, were tested with green-synthesized AgNPs plus colistin [13]. Colistin MICs ranged from 8 to 128 µg/mL; AgNP MICs from 0.07 to 37.5 µg/mL [13]. Checkerboards were synergistic or additive for every isolate; the lowest FIC index named in the extract is 0.31 for one isolate, and additive isolates sat at 1 [13]. Quorum-sensing gene expression fell in ATCC 27853 and isolate 354 under synergistic conditions, and scanning electron microscopy was used to support biofilm inhibition [13]. Murine L929 fibroblasts had an AgNP IC50 of 75 µg/mL and still more than 50% viability at 37.5 µg/mL [13]. These particles are not the same as chemically conjugated Col-AgNPs, so the two papers agree on the usefulness of combining silver with colistin, not on a shared pharmaceutical identity.

Vancomycin plus 10 nm AgNPs is a Gram-positive, cell-wall story. Against vancomycin-resistant *E. faecium*, AgNPs or rhodamine-labelled AgNPs alone had MIC 13.5 mg L−1 and vancomycin alone 512 mg L−1; the combination dropped to 3.38 plus 2 mg L−1, FIC 0.25 [12]. Imaging was interpreted as oxidative weakening of the wall plus intracellular particles deforming DNA [12]. Labelled and unlabelled silver MICs were matched at 13.5 mg L−1 against VRE, which is why the imaging particles can be discussed as relevant to the synergy number [12]. That FIC is strong synergy on the usual ≤0.5 rule, in vitro.

Aminoglycosides plus AgNPs close a loop toward tobramycin without substituting for an unread polymersome experiment. Across MDR strains, AgNPs cut the amikacin MIC about 22-fold [14]. In *E. coli* DH5α the AgNP MIC was about 3 µg/mL with an MBC/MIC of 2 in the reported experiment (three independent MIC runs) [14]. Host assays in that paper (mammalian cells, *C. elegans* lifespan, proteotoxicity) were negative in the authors’ hands [14]. Fold-drop in MIC is related to but not identical to an FIC index.

A 2021 review of antibiotic liposomes reminds the reader that some lipid antibiotic products already exist and that encapsulation can raise bioavailability and cut peripheral degradation [1]. Liposomes and AgNPs remain parallel answers to resistance [1]. Indicator strains, burn-unit isolates, and CDC AR Bank organisms are three distributions [11], [13], [14]. Synergy that appears in all three settings is more convincing than synergy in one, but the AgNP cores still differ (chemically conjugated, green-synthesized, commercial antimicrobial particles).

Silver is not inert at high concentration. L929 viability falling to 50% at 75 µg/mL AgNPs is a cell-line IC50, not a human no-effect level [13]. Hemolysis rising at 32 µg/mL is a blood-compatibility warning above the 16 µg/mL band emphasized for the colistin conjugate [11]. Those numbers matter because any nasal or wound use would have to sit below host damage while still exceeding biofilm-effective levels, which this sample does not measure in humans. This heading cannot show inhaled or nasal safety, licensed combination use, or superiority to a vesicle that actually carries tobramycin into a sinus biofilm.

## 8. Delivery to mucosa and biofilm

If the research question includes getting a particle to a mucosa, device geometry is not a side issue. Turbinate deposition of Otrivin in the Alberta Idealized Nasal Inlet (AINI) depended on actuation angle and temperature, with interactions between airflow and angle and between temperature and airflow [15]. Flows of 0, 7.5, 15, and 29 L/min were used to span breath-hold to heavy breathing [15]. Among 30°, 45°, and 60°, 30° favoured the turbinate region [15]. A design of experiments that crosses 21 °C and 35 °C and finds a temperature main effect plus a temperature–airflow interaction is saying that room-temperature inlets can rank products wrongly [15]. Brij coating to reduce bounce is a second in vitro choice that tries to mimic mucus without being mucus [15]. Sample size is not reported as a single N of human subjects; the work is an in vitro inlet study with repeats reported as mean ± SEM [15]. The product is a conventional nasal spray, not a liposome. The result still constrains any future nasal nanocarrier: if angle and heat change where a simple mist lands, a 100 nm vesicle spray cannot be assumed to match a beaker experiment.

A lung-biofilm nanocarrier review states that reaching the organ is not the same as penetrating extracellular polymer, and discusses inhalation as a local route [10]. That is the scientific gap left beside a 2026 polymersome concept for tobramycin and doripenem in chronic rhinosinusitis: the problem is real, and the specific vesicle result is not in the readable full texts. Empty charged nanoparticles that look antimicrobial are a known confounder; any later polymersome read must keep unloaded controls. Azimzadeh’s clinical-isolate plates and Guo’s secondary account of matrix barriers are the biofilm measurements that can actually be cited here [13], [10].

This heading cannot show liposomal or polymersomal deposition in AINI, human nasal pharmacokinetics, or a live-dead biomass figure for a dual-cargo polymersome under flow.

## Discussion

Charge, polymer length, and inlet temperature are three orthogonal knobs. Papers that turn only one knob should not be averaged with papers that turn another. The original studies in Table 1 are enough to name those knobs and, with Table 2, to show that a phase III thermosensitive doxorubicin study can still fail its primary endpoint [2]. They are not enough to pick a single winning vesicle for cancer and infection together. A fair next experiment names the knob it turns, reports n, and keeps an empty-carrier control when the particle itself can kill bacteria. Those controls belong in the same figure as the loaded vesicle. Empty AgNPs, empty pectin meshes, and empty polymersomes are not interchangeable blanks; each can change MIC or biomass on its own.

The included papers agree on a negative statement: accumulation is not efficacy. That statement was written for cancer liposomes in 2017 [3], [6]. Later reviews repeat it with dual-trigger and lipid-nanoparticle vocabulary [8], [4]. The original papers then disagree productively about the next move.

For pH-sensitive DOPE membranes, PEG is a tax on the trigger and on uptake [5], [7], [9]. For lyso-thermosensitive doxorubicin, PEG is part of a product that was tested in phase III and did not meet progression-free survival against ablation alone [2]. Those facts can both be true because the membranes, the drugs, and the clinical add-on (ablation duration) differ.

Pectin coating is not a stimulus. It is a change of surface charge and hydrodynamic size aimed at mucosa and the gut [16], [17]. Whether a 1 µm pectin–liposome object still behaves as a liposome in mucus is an open physical question that atomic-force images pose without answering in animals [16]. Sertraline liposomes show that anionic vesicles are also being explored for central-nervous-system-adjacent drugs, but the open paper here is behavioural, not a human exposure study [18].

AgNP–antibiotic work is the most quantitative synergy block in the included experiments, and it is still preclinical microbiology [11], [12], [13], [14]. FIC 0.25 against vancomycin-resistant enterococci is a strong in vitro signal [12]. It is not a liposome, not a nasal spray, and not a licensed combination. Colistin conjugates that lose to free colistin on MIC but beat AgNPs and look gentler on cells at 32 µg/mL occupy a different niche: a silver formulation that carries some peptide, not a peptide that needs silver to work at 1 µg/mL [11]. Biofilm gene readouts in clinical *P. aeruginosa* sit closer to the therapeutic problem of chronic infection [13], [10]. Whether a pH-disassembling polymer vesicle plus tobramycin and doripenem outperforms those AgNP combinations in a sinus-like flow system is not decided by the included articles.

The nasal inlet study is easy to dismiss if the only allowed objects are vesicles. It is on-topic if the question includes getting a particle to a mucosa [15]. Angle and temperature effects are large enough that a formulation group could improve a nanocarrier in a dish and lose the comparison in the nose by holding the bottle wrong or running the inlet cold [15].

Limits follow the designs. Several maps are older than 2021 because they still state the stimulus problem clearly. Some clinical papers on the same devices may exist outside this sample. Claims from HTML copies of two seed reviews and the molecular-dynamics paper were restricted to passages that were readable in those files. This is a narrative reading of the included studies, not a systematic review of every smart-liposome or AgNP paper. There is no shared primary endpoint across Table 1. Size in nanometres, percent calcein leak, percent tumour-growth inhibition, FIC, and turbinate deposition fraction do not go into one forest plot.

What to measure next is concrete. A pH-responsive vesicle or polymersome still needs a sinus biofilm flow model with empty-particle controls, because blank charged particles can look active [10]. A PEG titration (0, 0.6, 3 mol%, and a short PEG) on the same DOPE/CHEMS doxorubicin recipe in one mouse model would let the simulation, the dye study, and the 4T1 arm share an endpoint [5], [7], [9]. AINI deposition should be run on an actual liposomal or polymersomal spray at 35 °C, not only on Otrivin [15]. AgNP–antibiotic pairs should report host IC50 beside FIC on the same page and include a biofilm minimum biofilm inhibitory concentration, not only planktonic MIC [11], [12], [13]. Any thermosensitive liposome claim should pre-specify ablation or hyperthermia duration, because HEAT’s subgroup hinged on that clock [2].

## Conclusions

Stimulus-responsive liposomes exist to empty a vesicle where long circulation left it intact. In DOPE membranes that use acid as the cue, PEG can stabilize the lamellar state, reduce dye leak, reduce uptake, and, in one 4T1 doxorubicin study, reduce antitumour effect when it is added [5], [7], [9]. Polymer coats such as pectin change size and charge in a controllable microfluidic process but have not been shown here to improve a clinical endpoint [16]. AgNPs can synergize with vancomycin, colistin, or aminoglycosides in vitro, with FIC 0.25 as the strongest single index here, yet they remain a different platform from liposomes [11], [12], [13], [14]. Nasal deposition of a simple spray already depends on angle and temperature [15]. A PEGylated thermosensitive doxorubicin liposome plus ablation did not meet phase III progression-free survival in the HEAT trial as reviewed [2]. Chronic mucosal biofilm delivery by a dual-cargo polymersome remains an unmeasured comparison in the readable articles, not a completed story [10].

PEG mole fraction and PEG length belong on the same pH-sensitive doxorubicin recipe, with leak, uptake, and tumour volume on one protocol [5], [7], [9]. Unloaded-particle controls belong in any biofilm or checkerboard figure [13], [14], [10]. Inlet temperature and angle should be fixed before a nasal nanocarrier is ranked [15]. Time-at-temperature should be pre-specified whenever a heat-triggered vesicle is paired with ablation [2]. Those are unmeasured or under-specified endpoints, not a request for more slogans.
