# Review-article craft and human prose

Read this **before** drafting or rewriting `article.md` / `final-report.md`. Pair with `report-writing`. This file is about **genre and voice**. It is not a license to invent citations.

## What a review article is

A review article is a **secondary** paper. It does **not** report a new experiment. It summarises, compares, and interprets work already published (the included full texts). Its job is to teach a reader:

- what the main questions and tools in the field are (the Introduction must do this);
- recent results that change practice or mechanism;
- where studies agree, where they cannot be compared, and where they disagree;
- gaps that are scientific (missing population, missing PK, protocol without outcomes);
- what should be measured next.

It is **not** a primary research article (no new sample, no new p-values generated by the reviewer). It is **not** a survey that only lists papers. It is **not** a log of how the reviewer fetched PDFs.

Kinds that appear in this workflow:

- **Narrative / literature review** (default journal article here): thematic argument from included papers.
- **Systematic review / PRISMA**: methods may state criteria and counts; the Discussion still interprets science, not the download log.
- **Meta-analysis**: only if an included paper *is* a meta-analysis, or the user asked you to pool compatible numbers. Do not invent a pooled effect from incommensurable studies.

### Length

Unless the user asks for a short note, a full journal manuscript should be long enough to teach the background and treat every included study. Aim for **at least ~6,000 words of body text** (about 20 pages in a typical Word document: 12-pt serif, double-spaced, ~300 words/page). Do not pad with repetition, slogans, or process talk. Add length in the Introduction (teaching) and in per-paper methods/results.

### Introduction (mandatory teaching section)

A reader who is expert in an adjacent field but not this one must be able to follow §§3–N after the Introduction. Cover, with citations to included papers where they state it:

- physiology or technology the later sections assume (e.g. incretin effect, peptide gut barriers, what an automated insulin delivery system is);
- why the clinical problem exists (what current therapy already does and still fails);
- the debates the Discussion will return to;
- a last paragraph that states the **aim of this review** (manageable scope, recent work, what is in vs out).

Do not dump screening theatre into the Introduction. Do not write “this paper stands as a testament to…” or similar.

### Abstract

Topic; main empirical results with n and units from included papers; what those results do **not** imply; one implication for practice or next measurement. No workflow, no “OA export.”

### Discussion (see also report-writing)

Present **more than one reading** of the data when designs differ. State limitations of the **studies**, then how later work can build on them (not “previous work is incompetent”). Suggest extensions that a trialist or formulator could actually run. Keep bibliography as the trail to primary sources.

## Human prose (do not write like a chatbot)

LLMs regress to generic, promotional, statistically common phrasing. Scientific reviews need **specific facts** (dose, species, endpoint, n) and ordinary verbs (**is, are, was, has, showed, found**).

**Do not use** these as flourishes (one accidental “key” in “key findings” as a table header is fine; a paragraph stuffed with them is not):

- *stands as / serves as / functions as / marks a shift / setting the stage / indelible mark / evolving landscape / tapestry / testament / underscore / highlight / showcase / foster / bolster / delve / pivotal / crucial / robust / vibrant / groundbreaking / nestled / boasts / rich tapestry*
- *Not only X, but also Y* / *It is not just X, it is Y*
- *Despite its [praise], [topic] faces several challenges…* then a sunny closer
- *Additionally,* as a sentence opener stacked with *Moreover / Furthermore / Notably*
- Three-item adjective or phrase lists used as decoration (*clear, comprehensive, and robust*)
- Spaced em dashes used to punch up every other sentence. Prefer commas, parentheses, or a new sentence.
- Weasel crowds: *experts argue, observers have cited, industry reports* without naming the paper
- Superficial *-ing* tails that add no data: *…, highlighting its importance / underscoring its role / contributing to the broader landscape*
- Process talk in Abstract/Discussion/Conclusions: record counts, HTTP, Unpaywall, “PDFs we could open,” token estimates, file paths

**Do:**

- Prefer copulas and plain verbs: “X is a GLP-1 RA.” “The trial randomized 28 adults.”
- Put the number next to the claim.
- Name the paper, the design, and the endpoint.
- Let disagreement be incommensurable endpoints when that is the truth.
- Keep tone neutral. No travel-brochure or press-release diction.

After a draft, grep the article for the banned flourish list and cut hits that are not technical terms (e.g. “landscape” in ecology is fine; “the therapeutic landscape” is not).
